I think the logical problem of evil offers a very strong internal critique against the existence of the conventionally conceived monotheistic God (all good, all powerful, all knowing). I say this despite the fact that many scholars would rather now focus on the 'evidential argument of evil'. The latter holds that the existence of *gratuitous* evil—of there being present/past evils that God could have prevented without the loss of some greater good or of why he caused exactly this amount of evil rather than another amount, or why evil occur at the rate it does, or why do things like a deer burning in a forest fire occur—which make the *probability* of the existence of an omni-trait God highly unlikely.[^1] Some even consider the logical argument debunked following Plantinga's Free Will defence.

I disagree with this. I think the free will defence fails for several reasons, such as (a) the possibility of humans having morally significant free will in heaven yet cannot do any wrong, and also (b) God's status as a moral agent with free will yet being ontologically good and not able to do any wrong: both entailing that a world where creatures only do what is good is logically possible on conventional theism.

The purpose of this entry, however, is not to argue specifically for this case, but rather, it is to show, through the logical problem of evil, that the concept of 'evil' is incoherent under theism.

---

## The Incoherency of ‘Evil’ Under Theism

svg

The logical problem of evil is an argument that is meant to derive a logical contradiction from the existence of an all knowing, all good and all powerful Being (Tri-Omni) in a world where evil exists. If it goes through, then the existence of such a being would be logically impossible given that it entails a contradiction (it’s like maintaining that P and -P).

Below, I present the argument in a way which circumvents the defence of free will by understanding the ‘good’ in the orthodox way, namely, *what ought be done, all things considered (pro toto), in reference to God’s desires*. I argue that this leaves the theist faced with an unpalatable dichotomy: either give up on one of God’s Tri-Omni traits or deny that evil exists, as the concept of evil—construed as what, pro toto, ought *not* be done, in reference to God's desires—would then be incoherent given that an all good, all powerful, all knowing God is infallible with respect to his desires. The existence of evil, so construed, runs contrary to such a Being's infallibility.

---

As mentioned, let us conceive the ‘good’ as being analytically equivalent to the action guiding norm, which is to say, what pro toto ought be done. And so, 'evil' is just the opposite of good, that is, what pro toto ought *not* be done. Under orthodox theism, good/evil is construed in reference to God's desires.

So, if God instantiates and permits all actual and logically possible events, then, given his Omni properties (including His infallibility with respect to His desires), all events he instantiates and permits, including evil events, *are pro toto* good and therefore ought be done. That is to say, they are not evil. Thus, on theism, it is logically impossible for there to be any evil in the world since all evil events are *pro toto* good and *pro toto* ought to happen.

Otherwise, we derive a contradiction from God’s omni properties and Him permitting evil to occur.

Again: God is maximally good. God permits all events to occur. He maximally only does what ought be done because he is maximally good and always succeeds with respect to His desires. So all events are pro toto good and cannot be labelled evil.

To use an analogy, take my getting vaccinated against COVID-19. I abhor needles and I faint almost every time. Being pricked with a needle SUCKS and is something I consider evil. But if being pricked with a needle helps me survive the pandemic, then getting pricked with a needle is the least evil thing to do; it is pro toto good. So in this case, we say that getting vaccinated is good and not evil.

Now let's take the Holocaust as an example. The theist wants to say that the holocaust was an evil event, but maintain that it is not a *pro toto* evil because it is good for the sake of some other higher end, as God, being ontologically good, will only permit *pro toto* good events to occur (where good is coextensive to 'whatever conforms with God's desires/wills', and therefore ought be done).

So on theism, God must have permitted the Holocaust to occur for the sake of some greater good, and therefore the Holocaust is a *pro toto* good and should have happened. In fact, it would not make any sense to say that it is an evil event given that evil just means what does not conform with God's desires and pro toto ought not be done, but given God's omni-traits, clearly the Holocaust was actualised and therefore ought have been done and is in fact a good event (just like my getting vaccinated).

The idea is that, whatever the ‘greater good’ is, free will, the lessons learned, the ability to appreciate things etc., if it necessitates an evil event to first occur, then that evil pro toto ought be done, and is therefore a good event and not an evil event. If something is done for the sake of the ‘greater good’ (such as getting pricked by a needle) then it ought be done, and what ought be done is coextensive with what is good. On the theistic framework, then, the existence of evil is simply incoherent because all events God permits ought be done, all considered. The only way to get out of this problem, it seems to me, is for the theist to deny the existence of evil or give up on one of God's omni properties.

### **The Argument in Syllogistic Form**

svg

This argument takes the form of a *modus tollens*: If P then Q. Not Q. Therefore not P.

#### **Premise 1:**

svg

If God knows how to (omniscience), and is able to (omnipotence) actualise any logically possible world, then God will only actualise the best possible world (omni-benevolence).

*Derivation of P1.*

That is to say, given that God is infallible with respect to his desires, and given that he is maximally good, He will only actualise a maximally good world, in which 'good' is construed as ‘what pro toto ought be done’ in reference to His own desires.

There is a logically possible world where there is no evil, i.e., where there is nothing that pro toto ought not be done by God’s desires. In other terms, in an event where a given agent deliberates between what God pro toto desires to happen (e.g. worshipping Him) VS what God does not pro toto desire to happen (e.g. sinning); by definition, God would prefer the agent to act in accordance with what He pro toto desires to happen, and that would be the best possible world. It follows that we should be in a world where all agents act in accordance to what God pro toto prefers, otherwise God would be fallible with respect to his desires.

#### **Premise 2**:

svg

The world we are in is not the best possible world.

*Derivation of P2.*

But we are not in such a world given that evil occurs. Theists may object that we are in such a world: every event, every sin, every action is good because it is in accordance to God’s *pro toto* desires; a theist becoming atheist or the Holocaust or a deer burning in agony in a forest fire or a child dying of cancer are all what God *pro toto* desires. But if they are what God desires in a *pro toto* sense, then that just means that there cannot be any evil because literally everything is in accordance with God’s desires, i.e., are pro toto good, and what is good is what ought be done.

#### **Premise 3**:

svg

Therefore, either a God with those omni properties does not exist (he cannot actualise any logical possible world or does not want to actualise the best possible world) or evil does not exist.

The reductio ad absurdum to this position, however, is global scepticism, i.e. scepticism about the external world as a whole.
