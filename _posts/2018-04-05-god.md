## Evil

Suppose 'evil' is coextensive with an event that, **all things considered**, ought not occur.

Let:

**E** = an evil event occurs

**O(¬E)** = all things considered, E ought not occur

Now consider a tri-omni God. That is, an omniscient, omnipotent and omnibenevolent being.

## Argument

The argument can be stated thusly:

> **P1.** Evil(E) → O(¬E)  
>
> **P2.** An omnibenevolent being never knowingly permits an avoidable state of affairs that, all things considered, ought not occur.  
>
> **P3.** An omniscient being knows every actual evil and every consequence of preventing it.  
>
> **P4.** An omnipotent being can prevent any evil when doing so is logically possible.  
>
> **P5.** G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))  
>
> **P6.** O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E)) → ¬O(¬E)
>
> ---
>
> **C1.** G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → ¬O(¬E)  
>
> **C2.** G → ¬Evil(E)  
>
> **∴ ¬G ∨ ¬Evil(E)**


## Reasoning:

If E is evil, then:

> **Evil(E) → O(¬E)**

A maximally good being never knowingly permits an avoidable state of affairs that, all things considered, ought not occur. An maximally knowing being knows every actual instantiation of evil and every consequence of preventing it, while a maximally powerfull being can prevent any evil when it is logically possible to do so.

Therefore, if a God with these characteristics knowingly permits E, His permitting E must be justified all things considered:

> **G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))**

But now suppose the theist says both:

> **O(¬E)**

and:

> **O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))**

This creates a problem. If, **all things considered**, reality ought to be such that E does not occur, then a perfectly good and omnipotent God ought to prevent E. On the other hand, if God ought, all things considered, to permit E, then it cannot also be true, in the all-things-considered sense, that E ought not occur.

Therefore:

> **O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E)) → ¬O(¬E)**

And since:

> **Evil(E) ↔ O(¬E)**

it follows that:

> **G → ¬Evil(E)**

So the theist faces a dilemma:

> ## **¬G ∨ ¬Evil(E)**

Either the tri-omni God does not exist or no actual event is evil in the pro toto sense.

The typical rejoinder is that an evil may be permitted for some greater good (the lessons learned, the ability to appreciate things etc.). However this does not circumvent the argument. If permitting E is genuinely what ought to happen **all things considered** then E cannot simultaneously be something that **all things considered** ought not happen. It may be painful or horrific or bad or terrible (etc.) *in some respect*, but it certainly cannot any longer be *evil* in the stipulated pro-toto sense.

It is incoherent for the theist to label anything ultimately evil in a world knowingly and permissibly actualised by a perfectly good, perfectly knowing and perfectly powerful God.
