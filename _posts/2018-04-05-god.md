# Evil, Divine Permission and the All-Things-Considered Ought

Suppose “evil” means an event that, **all things considered, ought not occur**.

Let:

**E** = an evil event occurs

**O(¬E)** = all things considered, E ought not occur

Now consider a tri-omni God = omniscient, omnipotent and perfectly good.

## Argument

The argument can be stated formally:

> **P1.** Evil(E) → O(¬E)  
>
> **P2.** A perfectly good being never knowingly permits an avoidable state of affairs that, all things considered, ought not occur.  
>
> **P3.** An omniscient God knows every actual evil and every consequence of preventing it.  
>
> **P4.** An omnipotent God can prevent any evil when this prevention is logically possible.  
>
> **P5.** G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))  
>
> **P6.** O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E)) → ¬O(¬E)
>
> ---
>
> **C1.** G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → ¬O(¬E)  
>
> **C2.** G → ¬Evil(E)  
>
> **∴ ¬G ∨ ¬Evil(E)**

The reasoning is as follows.

If E is evil, then:

> **Evil(E) → O(¬E)**

A perfectly good being never knowingly permits an avoidable state of affairs that, all things considered, ought not occur. An omniscient God knows every actual evil and every consequence of preventing it, while an omnipotent being can prevent any evil when such prevention is logically possible.

Therefore, if God knowingly permits E, His permitting E must be justified all things considered:

> **G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))**

But now suppose the theist says both:

> **O(¬E)**

and:

> **O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))**

This creates a problem. If, **all things considered**, reality ought to be such that E does not occur, then a perfectly good and omnipotent God ought to prevent E. Conversely, if God ought, all things considered, to permit E, then it cannot also be true, in the all-things-considered sense, that E ought not occur.

Thus:

> **O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E)) → ¬O(¬E)**

And since:

> **Evil(E) ↔ O(¬E)**

it follows that:

> **G → ¬Evil(E)**

So the theist faces a dilemma:

> ## **¬G ∨ ¬Evil(E)**

Either the tri-omni God does not exist or no actual event is evil in the pro toto sense.

The typical rejoinder is that an evil may be permitted for some greater good. However this does not circumvent the argument. If permitting E is genuinely what ought to happen **all things considered**, then E cannot simultaneously be something that, **all things considered**, ought not happen. It may be painful or horrific *in some respect*, but it certainly is no longer evil in the stipulated pro-toto sense.

The logical problem therefore is a problem about the coherence of calling anything **ultimately evil** in a world knowingly and permissibly actualised by a perfectly good, omniscient and omnipotent God.
