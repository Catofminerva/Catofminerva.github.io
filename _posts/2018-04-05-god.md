## Evil

Suppose 'evil' is coextensive with an event that **all things considered** ought not occur.

Let:

**E** = an evil event occurs

**O(¬E)** = all things considered, E ought not occur

Now consider a tri-omni God. That is, an omniscient, omnipotent and omnibenevolent being.

## Argument

The argument can be stated thusly:

> **P1.** Evil(E) → O(¬E)  
>
> **P2.** An omnibenevolent being never knowingly permits an avoidable state of affairs that, all things considered, ought not occur.  
>
> **P3.** An omniscient being knows every actual evil and every consequence of preventing it.  
>
> **P4.** An omnipotent being can prevent any evil when doing so is logically possible.  
>
> **P5.** G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))  
>
> **P6.** O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E)) → ¬O(¬E)
>
> ---
>
> **C1.** G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → ¬O(¬E)  
>
> **C2.** G → ¬Evil(E)  
>
> **∴ ¬G ∨ ¬Evil(E)**



## Reasoning:

If E is evil, then:

> **Evil(E) → O(¬E)**

A maximally good being never knowingly permits an avoidable state of affairs that all things considered ought not occur. An all knowing being knows every actual evil occurrence and every consequence of preventing it, while an all powerful being can prevent any evil event when it is logically possible to do so.

Therefore, if a God with these characteristics knowingly permits E, His permitting E must be justified all things considered:

> **G ∧ Permit<sub>G</sub>(E) → O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))**

But now suppose the theist says both:

> **O(¬E)**

and:

> **O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E))**

This creates a problem. If **all things considered**, reality ought to be such that E does not occur, then a perfectly good and omnipotent God ought to prevent E. On the other hand, if God ought, all things considered, to permit E, then it cannot also be true, in the all-things-considered sense, that E ought not occur.

Therefore:

> **O(Permit<sub>G</sub>(E)) → ¬O(¬E)**

And since:

> **Evil(E) ↔ O(¬E)**

it follows that:

> **G → ¬Evil(E)**

Hence the theist faces a dilemma:

> ## **¬G ∨ ¬Evil(E)**

Either the tri-omni God does not exist or evil does not exist in the pro toto sense.

The typical rejoinder is that an evil may be permitted for some greater good (the lessons learned, the ability to appreciate things etc.). However this does not circumvent the argument. If permitting E is genuinely what ought to happen **all things considered** then E cannot simultaneously be something that **all things considered** ought not happen. It may be painful or horrific or bad or terrible (etc.) *in some respect*, but it certainly cannot any longer be *evil* in the stipulated pro-toto sense.

Consider the claim:

> ## “The Holocaust was genuinely evil, but God had morally sufficient reason to permit it because permitting it was necessary for some greater good.”

There are two possibilities.

If the greater good outweighs the reasons against permitting the Holocaust, such that God really ought all things considered to permit it, then the theist seemingly cannot also say that, all things considered, the Holocaust ought not have occurred.

They can still say:

- it was horrific;
- it involved enormous suffering;
- the perpetrators acted wickedly;
- there were powerful reasons against its occurrence;
- considered in itself, it really ought not occur.

But such statements are now pro tanto or agent-relative claims. They specifically are *not*:

> **O(¬E)**

in the stipulated pro tanto sense.

Under orthodox theism, it would make little sense to refer to 'evil' as an action-guiding normative category. The theist cannot coherently maintain that an actual event is evil in the all-things-considered sense that it ultimately ought not have occurred, while also maintaining that an omniscient, omnipotent and perfectly good God knowingly and permissibly actualised a world containing it.

Either:

> **O(¬E)**

in which case God apparently ought to have prevented E; or God's permission of E is ultimately justified, in which case:

**¬O(¬E).**

that is to say, that pro toto evil does not exist.
