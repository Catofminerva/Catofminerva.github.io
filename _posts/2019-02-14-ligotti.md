**UPDATE 26/08/2026:** Again, like most other blogposts here, this is a very old essay. It first appeared merely as a comment on Goodreads and then expanded into this essay. At the time of first writing the comment, back in 2019, I was quite depressed and was wrestling with existential questions and became enthralled with ‘pessimism’ as a genre of philosophy. I also found in Ligotti (and his teacher, Lovecraft) an obsession and became a ‘weird fiction’ enthusiast. Having perceived of being rejected outwardly, I retreated into myself, constructed an almost solipsistic world inwardly that world which was blocked from me externally, and became consumed with abstract philosophies and the highly emotional, dark and supernatural fiction. But on the bright side, this state of mind had introduced me to one of life’s most fulfilling pleasures; literature and philosophy.

Dickens describes my spirit at the time rather aptly in his famous opening line to A Tale of Two Cities:

> It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of light, it was the season of darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair.

Although many of those existential questions had been resolved now (or more accurately, dissolved) in 2026, and I am, as it were, ‘in a better place’ and no longer engage as much as I did in such literature and its hard questions, I nevertheless found upon reading the essay that it is may contain useful kernels of small T ‘truths’.

### **General Thoughts**

Thomas Ligotti’s *The Conspiracy Against the Human Race* is his first non-fiction book and arguably his magnum opus. It is among the most persuasive works I have encountered within the peculiar ‘antinatalist’ tradition. 

A word of caution: Ligotti presents a harrowing case of real-life, non-fictional horror. I adored it and believe others would too, but I hesitate to inflict its dark, brooding thoughts upon unsuspecting readers. However, for those who embrace the Ligottian credo ____ dive headfirst into this unnerving book. As I read, I found myself riveted to my seat, as if watching a gruesome thriller that entices me to shield my eyes while forbidding me from doing so. No self-help book will ever be able to mend the scars.

In Ligotti’s account, we’ve subconsciously constructed elaborate systems of meaning to evade the terror of a meaningless existence. Behold! We are nature’s anomalies, accidental byproducts, self-aware marionettes in a cosmic farce! And then he exposes the coping mechanisms we’ve devised to make life bearable, all manners of sophisticated commitments and distractions that function to to limit our awareness of what being alive entails. Alas, these systems prove fragile, as the absurdity of existence seeps into daily life so long as we remain self-conscious beings. Following Zapffe, Ligotti regards this excessive consciousness as an evolutionary misstep, disastrous for the beings who possess it.

The sentiments expressed by the book share particular motifs with the gang of solitary pessimists: Mainländer, Zapffe, Cioran and Schopenhauer. Their doctrines differ, but Ligotti finds ample material in their accounts of suffering, restless striving and the conceit beneath human passions. From Zapffe in particular he borrows his central idea that we make life tolerable by limiting consciousness, by excluding disturbing thoughts, occupying ourselves with distractions or finding security in religion and other commitments. Ligotti’s antinatalist prescription is to cease reproducing, to spare future beings the suffering that existence would impose upon them. He seems to derive a voyeuristic thrill from immersing us in these disquieting reflections and evoking the sensation of reading Schopenhauer as a horror writer.

Throughout the admittedly sometimes hyperbolic sketches, Ligotti still manages to make serious philosophical arguments. These are not altogether novel in kind, but the presentation is original, a kind of philosophical pessimism with a flavour of Lovecraftian cosmic horror. For example:

> For the rest of the earth’s organisms, existence is relatively uncomplicated. Their lives are about three things: survival, reproduction, death—and nothing else. But we know too much to content ourselves with surviving, reproducing, dying—and nothing else. We know we are alive and know we will die. We also know we will suffer during our lives before suffering—slowly or quickly—as we draw near to death. This is the knowledge we “enjoy” as the most intelligent organisms to gush from the womb of nature. And being so, we feel shortchanged if there is nothing else for us than to survive, reproduce, and die. We want there to be more to it than that, or to think there is. This is the tragedy: Consciousness has forced us into the paradoxical position of striving to be unself-conscious of what we are—hunks of spoiling flesh on disintegrating bones.

### **The Human Existential Paradox**

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e6bb61ce-1c5f-4963-a676-e34f5b1c430d_639x426.jpeg)  
*Woman At A Window*, 2012 by Richard Tuschman

Let’s examine more closely some of these themes. Ligotti’s main premise revolves around the existential paradox which afflicts the human race. In a strange twist in evolution, he argues, nature became aware of itself in an unfortunate species whose members must live with the knowledge that they will cease to exist. In his account, the optimists cope by endlessly distracting themselves and “artificially limiting the contents of consciousness”, while the pessimists condemn the predicament altogether.

This idea, that animals are privileged compared to us self-aware human beings because quote ‘reason’!! unquote, is a recurrent motif. For instance, Ligotti rejoices with Schopenhauer, who affirms that: 

> The capacity for feeling pain, increases with knowledge and reaches its highest degree in man.

There is also an echo in the opening passage of Simone de Beauvoir’s *The Ethics of Ambiguity*:

> Man knows and thinks this tragic ambivalence [death] which the animal and the plant merely undergo. A new paradox is thereby introduced into his destiny. “Rational animal”, “thinking reed”, he escapes from his natural condition without, however, freeing himself from it.

And of course, Peter Wessel Zapffe, Ligotti’s favourite philosopher, who describes how suffering expands into a fear of existence itself:

> Man became fearful of life itself – indeed, of his very being. Life – that was for the beast to feel the play of power, it was heat and games and strife and hunger, and then at last to bow before the law of course. In the beast, suffering is self-confined, in man, it knocks holes into a fear of the world and a despair of life.

Emil Cioran gives this preference for diminished consciousness one of his characteristically brilliant formulations:

> Better to be an animal than a man, an insect than an animal, a plant than an insect, and so on. Salvation? Whatever diminishes the kingdom of consciousness and compromises its supremacy.

And who can forget Philipp Mainländer? In his account, reason expands our desires far beyond immediate bodily needs: wealth, honour, power and fame give us more to strive for, more to lose and more reason to dread death, while:

> The animal basically follows its impulses, which are limited to hunger, thirst, the need to sleep and everything related to mating; it lives in a narrow sphere.

It is also one of the major themes of the show 'True Detective’ in which Rust Cohle, the main character of the show, says

> I think human consciousness is a tragic misstep in evolution. We became too self-aware.

The common thread here is that humans experience suffering and also reflect on it, though these thinkers differ over what follows. The experiences themselves range from mundane matters like stubbing your toe, waiting in traffic, itches, allergies and paper cuts, etcetera etcetera, to losing a loved one or facing the reality of your own death. There is an additional torment to this, however. We ruminate about why we suffer, what the purpose of our lives is, why we are here, ‘who am I’, etc.

I suspect that most humans ponders ‘the meaning of life’ at some point, however they cash out those terms. Yet existential reflection seems to rear its head more so in response to suffering than to good fortune. We can easily imagine a terminally ill patient lying awake at night asking why this has happened to them, while it is less natural to imagine a lottery winner agonising over why fortune favoured them. If this is the case, then it would suggest that existential reflection is caused, at least in part, by *the need to make sense of suffering*. Were human existence experienced as unambiguously or overwhelmingly positive, questions about its meaning would therefore occupy a less prominent role in our lives.

I suspect that Ligotti and his existential comrades might have preferred to be born as plants or flies, creatures spared from the burden of self-awareness.

### **Looking at Birds and Lilies with Kierkegaard**

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2b83c19a-8cd8-4769-b655-1b581c05df41_640x467.jpeg)

I think Ligotti would have done better with Kierkegaard than with Zapffe, although Zapffe certainly had his own account (sublimation) of how we make existence bearable. In *The Lily of the Field and the Bird of the Air*, Kierkegaard considers the birds and lilies of Matthew 6:26–29. The passage includes the familiar insistence that humans are of greater value, but its lesson concerns anxiety:

> And which of you by being anxious can add one cubit unto the measure of his life?

The birds neither sow nor reap, and the lilies neither toil nor spin, but God sustains them. Why, then, should humans spend their lives worrying? (Matthew 6:26–29)

Kierkegaard, being the brilliant existentialist he was, makes these apparently non-enviable forms of existence into our teachers. By looking at them and comparing their condition with ours, we need not turn immediately to melancholy over our self-awareness or congratulate ourselves on our privilege. There is a lot to be learned here.

Yes, we humans are different from birds and lilies, but there is also a portion of our existence that is not so different. Much of our attention is occupied by what agitates pessimists such as Ligotti: concern. We anticipate the future, revisit the past and compare ourselves with other people. We use concepts like ‘tomorrow’, ‘Monday’ and ‘winter’ to navigate our lives, until our immediate everyday is filled with concern about what is absent or has not happened.

But consider for a moment another way of dealing with existence. Kierkegaard’s bird waits without demanding to know when the appointed moment will arrive. It is silent. When the moment comes, it is ready for it.

The bird’s joy does not depend on being spared suffering. All of nature withers and perishes; it is red in tooth and claw. Nevertheless, Kierkegaard presents the bird and the lily as capable of unconditional joy. Through silence and obedience, they entrust their sorrow to God. They remain exposed to suffering but do not add the burden of anxious resistance to it.

How does one become like these artfully silent teachers? Kierkegaard’s answer is religious: learn to be silent before God, to obey and to rejoice. I am inclined to borrow something from this idea.

Here Henry David Thoreau gives us a parallel lesson: go for a nature walk. Leave the noise of human concern, get away for a while from the endless pursuits, desires and projects that overpopulate the cities. Thoreau does admit that we can walk into the woods bodily while remaining elsewhere in spirit. Getting outside is one thing; ceasing to rehearse the morning’s obligations is another.

Surrounded by nature, perhaps we can become less absorbed in our comparisons and projections, and more attentive to what is immediate, present and concrete. The lily and the bird become interesting in themselves. For a while, we are biological organisms among other organisms, with something to attend to besides the significance of our own existence.

Ligotti and the pessimists say that reflection makes our condition intolerable. Perhaps they would benefit from getting out of the house and going for a walk! Though I suspect Zapffe would recognise the excursion as another of those coping mechanisms we discussed earlier.(while Ligotti is a known recluse who refuses to be ever photographed: there's only one or two pictures of him on the internet and they're...a bit creepy)

### **Limiting the content of consciousness**

So if, according to Ligotti, humans have been dealt bad cards by evolution which gave us the ability to reflect on things that shouldn't be—never meant to be—reflected on, things like suffering and meaning, thus making us psychologically worse off, how do we deal with this? Ligotti's second premise is the idea, which he mostly borrows from Zapffe, that human beings "artificially limit the content of consciousness" in order to cope with the harshness and meaninglessness of self-conscious experience. The majority of what we do (working, buying things, believe in religious precepts, socialise, watch television etc.) is a kind of compulsive behaviour to ignore and deny an empty shell of an existence, according to Ligotti.

Schaupenhaur frequently describes this phenomenon of restless striving. He says, “All striving comes from lack, from a dissatisfaction with one's condition, and is thus suffering as long as it is not satisfied; but no satisfaction is lasting; instead, it is only the beginning of a new striving". Heidegger also describes a similar idea in his concept of 'Being-toward-death'. In it, Heidegger explains that the mode of everydayness is a mode of being that is evasive of death, that knows of its not-yet certainty and dreads it.

In *Untimely Meditations*, Nietzsche also spoke about this human predicament when he wrote

> "The objective of all human arrangements is through distracting one's thoughts to cease to be aware of life [...] For now he will have to descend into the depths of existence with a string of curious questions on his lips: why do i live? what lesson have I to learn from life? how have i become what I am and why do I suffer from being what I am?".

Freud was also probably one of the foremost thinkers on this topic with his theory of the unconscious. The psychologist Ernest Becker's *The* *Denial of Death* is a manifesto of this theme.

At any rate, if we boil down all action and thought as being consciousness limitation mechanisms then there is no escape; Ligotti himself by writing this book, me by reading it, are all, on his proposition, a means of sentience reduction. On this construal, existence is a string of consciousness limitations; consciousness suppression is the driving force behind all human psychological forces and the centre around which all human pursuits directly revolve. Even Mainländer's suicide can be conceived as such an act. Construed as such, it seems as though there is no way to actually falsify the theory, as anything and everything will be explained away as just another example of consciousness limitation; and if the phenomenon is literally everywhere then it is also nowhere.

Absent a way of corroborating it, it seems that our judgments of whether it is true or false comes down to aesthetic choices, personal temperaments, intuitions and conjectures. If this is so, and Ligotti seems to acknowledge as much in the passage;

> the eternal stalemate between optimists and pessimists, is that no possible formula can be established to measure proportions and types of hurt and happiness in the world

then why should one want to take such a gloomy outlook towards life? Why not just be agnostic? Seriously, is Ligotti just a depressed misanthrope? Of course, Ligotti anticipated these charges, writing:

> Depressing” is the adjective that ordinary folk affix to the life perspectives expressed by men such as Zapffe, Schopenhauer, and Lovecraft

And dismisses them as projections:

> Everyone wants to keep the door open on the possibility that our lives are not MALIGNANTLY USELESS. Even highly educated readers do not want to be told that their lives are an evolutionary contingency—and nothing else—and that meaning is not what people think it means

Basically, the claim is "There is no cosmic meaning and existence is riddled with suffering, so everything we do, as highly conscious beings, is to make us forget these facts". The prescription: ego death and stop reproducing. Then cue the charges: "You're just a depressed misanthrope". To which Ligotti replies "Yes! Yes I am, and how can anyone not be? I am depressed because I see how the world *really* is, instead of living happily ignorant".

At which point we agnostics, indifferent-ists, existentialists and pragmatists retort "But why should we care what the world *really* is, if it is, outside of what we can make of it? What difference does it make to practice? The only reason you’re sad about the state of existence is because you’re still, like the optimists, stuck in the world of human concern, of seeing humans as somehow different from the rest of the animals. This is a remnant concern of a tradition that told us that humans must expect there to be some cosmic meaning that justifies all”.

But who cares about the soundness of the argument, anyway? Contrast this book with something like *Straw Dogs: Thoughts on Humans and Other Animals* by John Gray which I thought I’d really like because there, too, I sympathise with the nihilistic themes but the book is written as a non-fiction treatise, making it very hard to overlook not so convincing ‘arguments’. Ligotti gets away with it because of his prose that almost bleeds into fiction.

### Why Not Pessimism?

Have I been totally initiated into the Ligotti cult of pessimism and borderline misanthropy? I would say no, like nature herself, I’d like to think that I’m indifferent to the evaluative status of existence; I don't see it as being either gloomy or bright, it just is. Ligotti alludes to the idea that being pessimist or an optimist is a matter of temperament or opinion, and nothing else. But, this seems to me to be a false dichotomy, can one, like the bird and the lily, simply live in the joy of the moment, that is, be indifferent (something similar to what the stoics preached)?

To answer the question posed in the title, 'why not pessimism?', I leave the readers with a passage from Simone de Beauvoir's *Ethics of Ambiguity*which we may conceive as a potent reply to Ligotti's nihilism and which I must disclose has since lifted his wizardly and otherwise mesmerising spell of nihilism from me:

> The nihilist attitude manifests a certain truth. In this attitude one experiences the ambiguity of the human condition. But the mistake is that it defines man not as a positive existence of a lack, but as a lack at the heart of existence, whereas the truth is that existence is not a lack as such. And if freedom is experienced in this case in the form of rejection, it is not genuinely fulfilled. The nihilist is right in thinking that the world possesses no justification and that he himself is nothing. But he forgets that it is up to him to justify the world and to make himself exist validly. Instead of integrating death into life, he sees in it the only truth of the life which appears to him as a disguised death. However, there is life, and the nihilist knows that he is alive. That’s where his failure lies. He rejects existence without managing to eliminate it. He denies any meaning to his transcendence, and yet he transcends himself. The man who delights in freedom can find an ally in the nihilist because they contest the serious world together, but he also sees in him an enemy insofar as the nihilist is a systematic rejection of the world and man, and if this rejection ends up in a positive desire for destruction, it then establishes a tyranny which freedom must stand up against

Basically (and on my interpretation), de Beauvoir says that Ligotti is 100% correct insofar as he describes the ambiguity of existence and the lack of cosmic meaning, but the conclusions which he draws from this are somewhat misguided (partly nihilistic, partly antinatalist—though De Beauvoir also chose never to have children but for altogether different reasons). Having an attitudinal stance (whether negative or positive) towards those facts is a relic of an onto-theological tradition which taught us that we should expect pre-written meanings and values written in the fabrics of the universe which frees us from the freedom to choose our own meanings and values. She says that it is a pseudo problem fabricated by previous traditions which presumed a transcendental meaning that exists “out there”. The solution is not to solve it but dissolve it. She asks: what if we draw the opposite conclusion that Ligotti and his friends do where they find their happiness and meaning in their own denial of the existence  of happiness and meaning, in a self-recursive way?
