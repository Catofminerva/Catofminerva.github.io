**UPDATE:** *Again, like most other blogposts here, this is a very old essay. It first appeared merely as a comment on Goodreads and then expanded into this essay. At the time of first writing the comment, back in 2021, I was quite depressed and was wrestling with existential questions and became enthralled with ‘pessimism’ as a genre of philosophy. I also found in Ligotti (and his teacher, Lovecraft) an obsession and became a ‘weird fiction’ enthusiast. Although many of those existential questions had been resolved now (or more accurately, dissolved) in 2026, and I am, as it were, ‘in a better place’ and no longer engage as much as I did in such literature and its hard questions, I nevertheless found upon reading the essay that it is may contain useful kernels of small T ‘truths’.*

### **General Thoughts**

Thomas Ligotti's *The Conspiracy Against the Human Race* is his first foray into non-fiction, and despite being his latest creation, it has rapidly cemented its place as his magnum opus. It is arguably the most persuasive work within the enigmatic 'antinatalist' tradition—so leave it to a Lovecraft-inspired, Weird fiction maestro to nearly convince me of one of the most peculiar views out there, all in a manner that is at once bone-chilling and yet too reasonable to dismiss out of hand.

A word of caution: Ligotti presents a harrowing case of real-life, non-fictional horror in a graphically forthright way that may leave the ill-prepared trembling with emotional distress as they come to grips with the unadulterated hideousness of sentient life. Recommending this book is a moral tightrope walk; I adored it and believe others would too, but I hesitate to inflict its dark, brooding thoughts upon unsuspecting readers.

However, for those who embrace the Ligottian credo—"when wisdom is bliss, 'tis folly to be ignorant"—then dive headfirst into this unnerving book. As I read, I found myself riveted to my seat, as if watching a gruesome thriller that both entices me to shield my eyes and yet, through the adrenaline rush, forbid myself from doing so. Ligotti methodically dismantles every edifice of cosmic meaning, every optimistic conviction I held dear and took for granted, every underlying presupposition, cradling them tenderly before reducing them to dust, leaving me with an enduring sense of enlightened melancholy and a bittersweet aftertaste. No self-help book will ever be able to mend the scars.

Ligotti guides the reader on a voyage beneath the façade of the fabricated reality we've subconsciously constructed to evade the terror of a meaningless existence. With surgical precision, he peels back the layers to reveal the void beneath: Behold! We are nature's anomalies, accidental byproducts, self-aware marionettes in a cosmic farce! And then he exposes the elaborate coping mechanisms we've devised to make life bearable—mechanisms we now accept as natural, rather than mere tools for reducing conscious suffering. Alas, however, these intricate systems prove fragile, as the absurdity of existence invariably seeps into daily life so long as we remain self-conscious beings, a trait Ligotti views as an evolutionary misstep never meant to occur.

The sentiments expressed by the book share particular motifs echoed by the gang of solitary pessimists: Mainländer, Zapffe, Cioran and Schopenhauer, and their seldom celebrated doctrines; distinct elements which conjure the gloomy image of the feeble human resolve to restlessly strive against the stringent laws of the cosmos; the conceit beneath all human passions, the absurdity of both mystical and rational systems and their equally instrumental function in consciousness limitation, the permanent state of suffering of every being, and its entailed prescription in ending all suffering by ending man as such; cease reproducing, reducing man back to unconscious nature where, no longer striving for anything, he cannot any longer be frustrated. Ligotti seems to derive a voyeuristic thrill from immersing us in these disquieting reflections and evoking the sensation of reading Schopenhauer as a horror writer.

Throughout the vibrant and admittedly sometimes hyperbolic sketches, Ligotti still manages to make somewhat sound and serious philosophical arguments which are, although not altogether novel in kind (he references many philosophers and thinkers), at any rate unique in presentation and prose. The novelty is in discussing these niche academic philosophical themes and then sprinkling them with a flavour of Lovecraftian cosmic horror. This element gives Ligotti permission to be as blunt as he wants. For example:

> For the rest of the earth’s organisms, existence is relatively uncomplicated. Their lives are about three things: survival, reproduction, death—and nothing else. But we know too much to content ourselves with surviving, reproducing, dying—and nothing else. We know we are alive and know we will die. We also know we will suffer during our lives before suffering—slowly or quickly—as we draw near to death. This is the knowledge we “enjoy” as the most intelligent organisms to gush from the womb of nature. And being so, we feel shortchanged if there is nothing else for us than to survive, reproduce, and die. We want there to be more to it than that, or to think there is. This is the tragedy: Consciousness has forced us into the paradoxical position of striving to be unself-conscious of what we are—hunks of spoiling flesh on disintegrating bones.

### **The Human Existential Paradox**

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e6bb61ce-1c5f-4963-a676-e34f5b1c430d_639x426.jpeg)  
*Woman At A Window*, 2012 by Richard Tuschman

Let's examine closer some of these themes alluded to above. Ligotti’s main premise centres around this existential paradox which inflicts the human race and differentiates it from the rest of the species. In a strange twist in evolution, he argues, nature had become aware of itself and made this manifest in an unfortunate species, whose current active members, faced with the prospects of their own, literally inconceivable, because inexperienced, non-existence, cope through either endlessly distracting themselves and “artificially limiting the contents of consciousness” (the optimists) or condemn altogether the state of their existence (the pessimists).

This (animals are privileged compared to us self-aware human beings because quote ‘reason’!! unquote) is a recurrent motif shared by many other authors. For instance, Ligotti rejoices with Schopenhauer who affirmes that

> The capacity for feeling pain, increases with knowledge and reaches its highest degree in man.

It also echoes a statement Simone de Beauvoir makes in her opening passage of *The Ethics of Ambiguity*, in which she says

> Man knows and thinks this tragic ambivalence [death] which the animal and the plant merely undergo. A new paradox is thereby introduced into his destiny. “Rational animal”, “thinking reed”, he escapes from his natural condition without, however, freeing himself from it.

And of course, Peter Wessel Zapffe, Ligotti’s favourite philosopher, who remarked that

> Man became fearful of life itself – indeed, of his very being. Life – that was for the beast to feel the play of power, it was heat and games and strife and hunger, and then at last to bow before the law of course. In the beast, suffering is self-confined, in man, it knocks holes into a fear of the world and a despair of life.

Emil Cioran also once noted in one of his brilliant aphorisms that it is

> Better to be an animal than a man, an insect than an animal, a plant than an insect, and so on. Salvation? Whatever diminishes the kingdom of consciousness and compromises its supremacy.

And who can forget Philipp Mainlander, that terribly disturbed soul who wrote:

> The animal basically follows its impulses, which are limited to hunger, thirst, the need to sleep and everything related to mating; it lives in a narrow sphere. To the human being, on the other hand, life comes to him through reason, in the form of wealth, women, honor, power, fame, etc., which fuels his will to live, his yearning to live. Reason makes satisfaction, artificially, a refined enjoyment. Thus death is detested with all one's soul and the mere mention of such a word tormentingly contracts the hearts of the majority, and the fear of death turns into anguish of death and despair, when human beings cast their eyes upon it. On the contrary, life is loved with passion. Accordingly, in the human being the will to die - the innermost impulse of his essence - is no longer concealed by the will to live as simply as in the animal, but disappears completely into the depths, from where it only manifests itself, from time to time, as a deep longing for tranquility.

It is also one of the major themes of the show 'True Detective’ in which Rust Cohle, the main character of the show, says

> "I think human consciousness is a tragic misstep in evolution. We became too self-aware. Nature created an aspect of nature separate from itself. We are creatures that should not exist by natural law. We are things that labor under the illusion of having a self, this accretion of sensory experience and feelings, programmed with total assurance that we are each somebody, when in fact everybody's nobody. I think the honorable thing for our species to do is to deny our programming. Stop reproducing. Walk hand in hand into extinction. One last midnight, brothers and sisters opting out of a raw deal".

All these thinkers share a common thread that with self-awareness, humans not only experience things but also reflect on them. A significant portion of these experiences, some argue most of them, are less than pleasant. This includes mundane matters like stubbing your toe, waiting in traffic, having unfulfilled desires, disliking your appearance, getting things like itches and allergies, menopause and periods, headaches and paper cuts etcetera etcetera (see David Benatar for a [list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Human_Predicament) of these), and the less mundane, like having a love one die, facing the reality of one's own death and the dissatisfaction with one's self. We do not only have these experiences but we reflexively think about them, we ruminate about why gratuitous suffering exists, what is the purpose of our lives, why are we here, ‘who am I’ etc.

It has long been argued by many psychologists that increase in self-awareness leads to both an increase psychological distress and well-being. I suspect that most humans ponders ‘the meaning of life’ at some point, however they cash out those terms. Yet existential reflection seems to rear its head more so in response to suffering than to good fortune. We can easily imagine a terminally ill patient lying awake at night asking why this has happened to them, while it is less natural to imagine a lottery winner agonising over why fortune favoured them. If this is the case, then it would suggest that existential reflection is caused, at least in part, by *the need to make sense of suffering*. Were human existence experienced as unambiguously or overwhelmingly positive, questions about its meaning would therefore occupy a less prominent role in our lives.

I suspect that Ligotti and his existential comrades might have preferred to be born as plants or flies, creatures spared from the burden of self-awareness.

### **Looking at Birds and Lilies with Kierkegaard**

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2b83c19a-8cd8-4769-b655-1b581c05df41_640x467.jpeg)

I think Ligotti would have done better with Kierkegaard than with Zapffe (although [sublimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Wessel_Zapffe) was one of Zapffe's core tenets). In *The Lily of the Field and the Bird* *of the Air*, Kierkegaard quotes Matthew 6:24 who runs the tired old theme of human beings as privileged with the faculty of reason *vis-a-vis* the dumb bird of the air and the lily of the field:

> Behold the birds of the heaven, that they sow not, neither do they read, nor gather into barns; and your heavenly Father feedeth them all. Are not ye of much more value than they? And which of you by being anxious can add one cubit unto the measure of his life? And why are ye anxious concerning raiment? Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; and they toil not, neither do they spin: yet I say unto you, that even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these

Kierkegaard, being the brilliant existentialist he was, was able to salvage a use to these biblical passages by deriving an allegory in which the bird and the lily stand for important human lessons other than the 'humans are privileged' theme which God seemed to have intended. After all the most profound form of speech is allegorical. By looking at these apparently non-enviable forms of existence and comparing them with the condition of humans, instead of turning into melancholy and despair or happiness and privilege, Kierkegaard tells us there’s a lot to be learned here.

Yes, we humans are different from birds and lilies, but there is also a portion of our existence that is not so different. Most of our existence is spent in another portion which agitates pessimist thinkers such as Ligotti, namely that of concern. Human concern largely comes from our abstract reasoning of time; past, present and future. We use these concepts, ‘tomorrow’, ‘Monday’, 'winter' etc., to navigate our lives and our immediate everyday is filled with concern about such abstractions. A consequence of this, says Kierkegaard, is that we start making relational comparisons; of comparing ourselves with other people and things. But try for a moment to sublimate that concern into another mode of existence, one that lay outside the realm of human concern.

> Let us now look more closely at the lily and the bird from whom we are to learn. The bird keeps silent and waits: it knows, or rather it fully and firmly believes, that everything takes place at its appointed time. Therefore, the bird waits, but knows that it is not granted to it to know the hour and the day; therefore it keeps **silent**.

Kierkegaard tells us that, like the bird up on the tree, not caring about anything but the present, we too, can be fully present in the joy of the moment and cease worrying about what will happen tomorrow and the next day. He says that the bird is not joyful because she does not suffer while humans do. No, she does suffer, more than humans do. All of nature withers, perishes, all of it is red and tooth and claw. But at the same time, nature is the epitome of the eternal moment, like a picture forever suspended by the shutters' click.

> With the help of unconditional silence and unconditional obedience […] they cast all their sorrow away. At that very instant […] it is unconditionally joyful. Marvelous dexterity! To be able to take hold of all one’s sorrow at once, and then to be able to cast it away from oneself so dexterously and hit the mark with such certainty! Yet this is what the lily and the bird do, and therefore they are unconditionally joyful at that very instant.

How does one be like the artfully *silent* teachers that are the bird and the lily? Kierkegaard tells us that it is by getting closed to God. Henry David Thoreau secularised this notion by telling us to simply go on a nature walk. Leave the noise of anthropological concern, get away from the realm of endless pursuits, desires, passions, and human projects that overpopulates the cities. Surrounded by nature, we abjure the realm of human concern—the realm of temporality and abstractness—and are *thrown* into the portion of existence that is immediate, present and concrete, a realm where the lily and the bird thrive in. No comparisons to other things, no temporal concern, no planning, striving and projecting. Here we are back to our roots, biological organisms that are no different from the rest of the organisms. He says:

> The silent bird frees itself from what makes the suffering more burdensome; from the misunderstood sympathy of others; frees itself from makes the suffering last longer; from all the talk of suffering; frees itself from what makes the suffering into something worse than suffering; from the sin of impatience and sadness.

Ligotti and the pessimists say that we are condemned in the realm of human concern, in the realm of reflection. Perhaps they would benefit from getting out of the house and go for a walk! (Ligotti is a known recluse who refuses to be ever photographed: there's only one or two pictures of him on the internet and they're...a bit creepy)

### **Limiting the content of consciousness**

So if, according to Ligotti, humans have been dealt bad cards by evolution which gave us the ability to reflect on things that shouldn't be—never meant to be—reflected on, things like suffering and meaning, thus making us psychologically worse off, how do we deal with this? Ligotti's second premise is the idea, which he mostly borrows from Zapffe, that human beings "artificially limit the content of consciousness" in order to cope with the harshness and meaninglessness of self-conscious experience. The majority of what we do (working, buying things, believe in religious precepts, socialise, watch television etc.) is a kind of compulsive behaviour to ignore and deny an empty shell of an existence, according to Ligotti.

Schaupenhaur frequently describes this phenomenon of restless striving. He says, “All striving comes from lack, from a dissatisfaction with one's condition, and is thus suffering as long as it is not satisfied; but no satisfaction is lasting; instead, it is only the beginning of a new striving". Heidegger also describes a similar idea in his concept of 'Being-toward-death'. In it, Heidegger explains that the mode of everydayness is a mode of being that is evasive of death, that knows of its not-yet certainty and dreads it.

In *Untimely Meditations*, Nietzsche also spoke about this human predicament when he wrote

> "The objective of all human arrangements is through distracting one's thoughts to cease to be aware of life [...] For now he will have to descend into the depths of existence with a string of curious questions on his lips: why do i live? what lesson have I to learn from life? how have i become what I am and why do I suffer from being what I am?".

Freud was also probably one of the foremost thinkers on this topic with his theory of the unconscious. The psychologist Ernest Becker's *The* *Denial of Death* is a manifesto of this theme.

At any rate, if we boil down all action and thought as being consciousness limitation mechanisms then there is no escape; Ligotti himself by writing this book, me by reading it, are all, on his proposition, a means of sentience reduction. On this construal, existence is a string of consciousness limitations; consciousness suppression is the driving force behind all human psychological forces and the centre around which all human pursuits directly revolve. Even Mainländer's suicide can be conceived as such an act. Construed as such, it seems as though there is no way to actually falsify the theory, as anything and everything will be explained away as just another example of consciousness limitation; and if the phenomenon is literally everywhere then it is also nowhere.

Absent a way of corroborating it, it seems that our judgments of whether it is true or false comes down to aesthetic choices, personal temperaments, intuitions and conjectures. If this is so, and Ligotti seems to acknowledge as much in the passage;

> the eternal stalemate between optimists and pessimists, is that no possible formula can be established to measure proportions and types of hurt and happiness in the world

then why should one want to take such a gloomy outlook towards life? Why not just be agnostic? Seriously, is Ligotti just a depressed misanthrope? Of course, Ligotti anticipated these charges, writing:

> Depressing” is the adjective that ordinary folk affix to the life perspectives expressed by men such as Zapffe, Schopenhauer, and Lovecraft

And dismisses them as projections:

> Everyone wants to keep the door open on the possibility that our lives are not MALIGNANTLY USELESS. Even highly educated readers do not want to be told that their lives are an evolutionary contingency—and nothing else—and that meaning is not what people think it means

Basically, the claim is "There is no cosmic meaning and existence is riddled with suffering, so everything we do, as highly conscious beings, is to make us forget these facts". The prescription: ego death and stop reproducing. Then cue the charges: "You're just a depressed misanthrope". To which Ligotti replies "Yes! Yes I am, and how can anyone not be? I am depressed because I see how the world *really* is, instead of living happily ignorant".

At which point we agnostics, indifferent-ists, existentialists and pragmatists retort "But why should we care what the world *really* is, if it is, outside of what we can make of it? What difference does it make to practice? The only reason you’re sad about the state of existence is because you’re still, like the optimists, stuck in the world of human concern, of seeing humans as somehow different from the rest of the animals. This is a remnant concern of a tradition that told us that humans must expect there to be some cosmic meaning that justifies all”.

But who cares about the soundness of the argument, anyway? Contrast this book with something like *Straw Dogs: Thoughts on Humans and Other Animals* by John Gray which I thought I’d really like because there, too, I sympathise with the nihilistic themes but the book is written as a non-fiction treatise, making it very hard to overlook not so convincing ‘arguments’. Ligotti gets away with it because of his prose that almost bleeds into fiction.

### Why Not Pessimism?

Have I been totally initiated into the Ligotti cult of pessimism and borderline misanthropy? I would say no, like nature herself, I’d like to think that I’m indifferent to the evaluative status of existence; I don't see it as being either gloomy or bright, it just is. Ligotti alludes to the idea that being pessimist or an optimist is a matter of temperament or opinion, and nothing else. But, this seems to me to be a false dichotomy, can one, like the bird and the lily, simply live in the joy of the moment, that is, be indifferent (something similar to what the stoics preached)?

To answer the question posed in the title, 'why not pessimism?', I leave the readers with a passage from Simone de Beauvoir's *Ethics of Ambiguity*which we may conceive as a potent reply to Ligotti's nihilism and which I must disclose has since lifted his wizardly and otherwise mesmerising spell of nihilism from me:

> The nihilist attitude manifests a certain truth. In this attitude one experiences the ambiguity of the human condition. But the mistake is that it defines man not as a positive existence of a lack, but as a lack at the heart of existence, whereas the truth is that existence is not a lack as such. And if freedom is experienced in this case in the form of rejection, it is not genuinely fulfilled. The nihilist is right in thinking that the world possesses no justification and that he himself is nothing. But he forgets that it is up to him to justify the world and to make himself exist validly. Instead of integrating death into life, he sees in it the only truth of the life which appears to him as a disguised death. However, there is life, and the nihilist knows that he is alive. That’s where his failure lies. He rejects existence without managing to eliminate it. He denies any meaning to his transcendence, and yet he transcends himself. The man who delights in freedom can find an ally in the nihilist because they contest the serious world together, but he also sees in him an enemy insofar as the nihilist is a systematic rejection of the world and man, and if this rejection ends up in a positive desire for destruction, it then establishes a tyranny which freedom must stand up against

Basically (and on my interpretation), de Beauvoir says that Ligotti is 100% correct insofar as he describes the ambiguity of existence and the lack of cosmic meaning, but the conclusions which he draws from this are somewhat misguided (partly nihilistic, partly antinatalist—though De Beauvoir also chose never to have children but for altogether different reasons). Having an attitudinal stance (whether negative or positive) towards those facts is a relic of an onto-theological tradition which taught us that we should expect pre-written meanings and values written in the fabrics of the universe which frees us from the freedom to choose our own meanings and values. She says that it is a pseudo problem fabricated by previous traditions which presumed a transcendental meaning that exists “out there”. The solution is not to solve it but dissolve it. She asks: what if we draw the opposite conclusion that Ligotti and his friends do where they find their happiness and meaning in their own denial of the existence  of happiness and meaning, in a self-recursive way?
