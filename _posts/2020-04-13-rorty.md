### UPDATE 27/08/2026: 
This essay was first written in 2020, when I was around 23 years old. Reading it again now in late 2026, I scarcely recognise my younger self. It is incredible how differently I see things now compared to then. I no longer identify as Rorty’s moderate leftist, and would eschew a lot of the hostility towards the so-called 'radical' left, and marxists in particular. In fact, I am more inclined today towards [anarchism] (https://margaretkilljoy.substack.com/p/anarchism-and-its-misunderstanders) than I am towards the middle-of-the-road leftist you encounter here. 

I was then consumed by the liberal idea that the advent of a socialist or marxist or at any rate 'far left' order will necessarily lead to a violent bloody revolution. So incremental reform is the only solution for change. But I think that human beings could in fact come to the resolve that the present needs radical change without the need for mass violence. I think once they eventually come face-to-face with the present violence of modern civilisation and capitalism, they would willy nilly come to this resolve. If structures of property, wage labour, economic inequality, the loss of autonomy, are things that are presently brushed off, then surely the forthcoming climate change, top soil erosion and the loss of biodiversity on a massive scale, would force them to come to this resolve without the need of revolutionary violence. Modern civilisation, and capitalism as its form of economy, depends on burning through our survival conditions. The status quo is unhinged and an advocacy for mass genocide.

My quibbling in this essay about how exactly will the means of productions be collectivised without reinstating power-based hierarchical structures seems to be a peripheral concern when compared to the looming ecological collapse that will inevitably force our hand. I abjure  the idea that one must have a ready blueprint before advocating for radical change.

There are, however, much else that I do still agree with my former self. I have cut out a good chunk of the essay, which was embarrassing to read, so you probably won't encounter my younger liberal self. Well, I had better stop here before this turns into another mini-essay!

## **The Old Reformist Left**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b6ca5bb9-b6b9-42e0-b5af-696675c0ba12_1973x1312.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5sgt!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb6ca5bb9-b6b9-42e0-b5af-696675c0ba12_1973x1312.jpeg)

*Achieving Our Country* is a series of lectures delivered by American Pragmatist philosopher Richard Rorty in 1997, in which he reflects on American Leftism in the past century. In one of the lectures, *American National Pride: Whitman and Dewey*, Rorty laments the state of the American Left of his time and its apparent abandonment of early Leftist ideals pertaining to hope and patriotism, which, he says, have been substituted by excessive and abstract theorising, self-hatred, and resigned nihilism. He contends that the academic Left has turned its back on "real leftist politics" to indulge in esoteric cultural and identity pursuits from the comfort of their ivory towers, content with passively condemning "the system," "capitalism," "neoliberalism," and "neocolonialism," rather than proposing tangible, specific social policies to alleviate human suffering.

Throughout these intellectually stimulating lectures, Rorty nostalgically revisits what he refers to as "the old reformist Left"—a patriotic American Left inspired by the likes of John Dewey and Walt Whitman, espousing a rhetoric of commonality and communitarianism rather than merely respecting and recognising differences. This Left admits the constructive nature of national identity while nevertheless acknowledging and espousing its ability for solidarity and as a basis of appeal to justice (“fellow Americans”) and would find it politically correct to say such now passé cliches such as "I don't see colour; I only see you as an American", not as a way to hand-wave current injustices or elude the fact that such colour blindness does not currently exist, but as a way to appeal “as Martin Luther King did, to the whites’ sense of justice to their fellow Americans”, instead of preaching that whites and blacks have separate cultural identities and not the same, American, one, which would include the legacies of the Rosa Parks, the Fredrick Douglas’ and the MLKs as well as the Lincolns and the Jane Adams’ as part of its imagined mythos.

This American narrative, as imagined by Dewey and Whitman, is steeped in a sense of solidarity and fraternity and grounded in the human hope that the future will surpass the past instead of an idea of a Libretto of History. This is an America where people of diverse backgrounds come together to create a unified culture that transcends its individual components. Though there exists another, darker narrative of America, one founded on bloodshed and brutality, and Rorty concedes that both accounts are historically accurate, but he argues that dwelling on the latter with self-disgust is a vestige of the theological concept of 'sin.' He suggests that we view these past heinous acts as failures of imagination by previous generations, who operated within a historically defined framework of justice. The slave trade, the massacre of Native Americans, and foreign wars exemplify this framework's shortcomings, as the America that institutionalised lynching failed to uphold its proclaimed ideals of liberty and equality.

Yet, Rorty posits that ‘America’ is a floating signifier, an open-ended project, a work in progress. While American forebears (previous participants in this project) failed to empathise with and walk in the shoes of disenfranchised women, slaves, Vietnamese children, and others, yet condemning this ongoing endeavour as irredeemably wicked only leads to the stagnation of the project and its ideal. This mindset, prevalent today in what Rorty calls ‘the cultural left’, fosters a milieu of passive observation, theorising, and castigation, with the misguided hope that the project will one day be torn asunder, by a bloody revolution if need be, and start afresh with a blank page.

To Rorty, the ‘cultural left’ overlook that America is a project grounded on egalitarian ideals and that this therefore calls for a patriotic narrative of national solidarity to build it. Worse still, they incessantly meta-theorise on the past to the determinant of the existing human suffering occurring right this moment. They fail, in his view, in responding to the deeper ailments that agitate the working classes, those whose great suffocated cries are yet unheard by an 'identity-politics' obsessed Left. The early Left to which Rorty appeals found one of its later expressions in Martin Luther King’s attempt to make racial justice part of a common American project. King’s appeal was immanent through and through, such that America is to be condemned for failing to realise its own professed ideals which "the architects of our republic" signed a "promissory note...that all men – yes, black men as well as white men" will receive justice. America in his lights was not dismissed as an irredeemable project. In the March on Washington speech, he hoped that “one day this nation will rise up and live out the true meaning of its creed”, and imagined the sons of former slaves and former slave owners sitting together “at the table of brotherhood”.

MLK did not condemn America as unforgivable. He argued for a better America within the framework of its own national ideals and by appeals to national solidarity. Rorty is not entirely isolated in this point and had cited esteemed American educator Sheldon Hackney who similarly called for a "national conversation" regarding American identity. Nationalism here is taken to be imbued with progressive liberal notions of fraternity in the spirit of Mazzini and untarnished by the corrosive influence of fascists and race supremacists who had grounded their thought in early ideas such as that of Fichte's 18th-century populist propaganda on the Urvolk and the 'National Will' and Francis Galton's Social Darwinism. Rorty was unequivocally appalled by such currents.

Fast forward more than two decades, and one might suspect that many Americans today would find Rorty's insights just as relevant to the current Left—a Left they perceive as fixated on Twitter skirmishes, factionalism, infighting, sanctimony, and purity testing, favouring abstract politics over tangible action and initiatives. And indeed, many have: these prophetic and insightful the \*Lectures\* have recently garnered considerable attention from academics and the public alike, with scores of reviews overpopulating the internet and exalting this relatively obscure figure as a prophet who predicted the election of Donald Trump.

But one prophetic passage has captured the minds of so many contemporaries like no other. After speculating on the dire consequences that globalisation will have on the working classes due to the outsourcing of cheaper foreign labour, and about what he took to be the inexorable rise of right-wing populist movements this will consequently bring about, Rorty observes:

> At that point, something will crack. The nonsuburban electorate will decide that the system has failed and start looking around for a strongman to vote for—someone willing to assure them that, once he is elected, the smug bureaucrats, tricky lawyers, overpaid bond salesmen, and postmodernist professors will no longer be calling the shots…[and] people will wonder why there was so little resistance to his evitable rise...where, they will ask, was the American Left? […]
>
> One thing that is likely to happen is that the gains made in the past fifty years by black and brown Americans, and by homosexuals will be wiped out. Jocular contempt for women will come back in fashion. The words “nigger” and “kike” will again be heard in the workplace. All the sadism which the academic Left tried to make unacceptable to its students will come flooding back. All the resentment which badly educated Americans feel about having manners dictated to them by college graduate will find an outlet (Rorty 1998:89-90).

## **What Kind of Leftist Was Rorty?**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/29cb35a9-bb3b-43c2-9f09-07d61464db80_1920x1280.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ArYZ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F29cb35a9-bb3b-43c2-9f09-07d61464db80_1920x1280.jpeg)

While the explanation might be a bit wonky, it is nevertheless an accurate prediction of the Donald Trump election which saw America slip into a far-right populist government and almost ended with an attempted coup d'état that aimed at [overturning a democratic election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack).

The premise behind Rorty’s prediction might at first sight make it appear as though he is sympathetic to the anti-globalisation and pro-protectionism talking points common to conservative nationalist parties. But we must also remember that the Left had traditionally held similar sentiments, but unlike the Right, most do not reject globalisation tout court (or perhaps more accurately, globalisation construed as international integration and the free movement of peoples). They reject  corporate globalisation, precisely what Rory is protesting.

Some contemporary scholars do, in fact, cautiously affirm Rorty's predictive explanation for Trump's rise. Sociologist Michael Kimmel, for example, asserts that the ascent of Trumpian populism stems from a visceral sense of "righteous indignation that the government is screwing 'us,'" and that the benefits many Americans once considered themselves "entitled to have been snatched away by unseen forces, larger and more powerful … [They] feel [themselves] to be the heir to a great promise, the American Dream, which has turned into an impossible fantasy for the very people who were supposed to inherit it." Exit polling [data](https://www.sueddeutsche.de/politik/trumps-vordenker-wer-amerika-nach-rechts-rueckte-1.3648311) also aligns with Rorty's portrayal of an economically and socially disenchanted demographic, predominantly from the lower and working classes, mobilised by a "strongman."

When I first read this book, I was unfamiliar with Rorty's other work, and so in my naivety, I found myself sometimes having difficulty not to compare Rorty's seemingly nationalistic polemics to that which is espoused by contemporary conservatives and ultra nationalists, just by sheer association of concepts (‘he's a patriot and he's against one construal of identity-politics, surely he’s therefore a chauvinist’). However, a closer reading and a familiarisation with Rorty's other works, which so often champions ‘moral progress’ in the sense of extending our sensibilities and including more and more individuals within our framework of moral consideration, makes this very much doubtful and comparing him with today's conservatives would be misleading if not outrageous.

For Rorty this is a notorious liberal predicament which leaves one stuck between two extremes. Rorty is a leftist when he speaks of dreams and utopias, of a classless and casteless society, but he was also absolutely repelled to all that was abstract, theoretical, general, absolute, eternal, universal; and he was drawn to the concrete, sharp and achievable. He admired Marx and other political philosophers who are great in “helping us envisage a social democratic utopia”,  but, to him, they had no use in giving us “new political weapons or freeing us from old political illusions”.

Others from the right liked to believe that there is a straight road leading from Marx to the gulag, but Rorty did not appear to share this belief, nor did he believe there to be any clear-cut precursors from one individual thinker to out-right totalitarianism, and certainly not Marx who championed the cause of the poor and the overworked under the arms of a despotic industrialism. While the right were content with calling Rorty a moral nihilist and dangerous relativist who is corrupting the minds of their children, he was never radical 'enough' for the left either, and this made him a particularly despised figure and lead him to reclaim the title ‘bourgeoise liberal’, partly out of spite.

During the *Lectures*, Rorty calls on his fellow Americans to “rejoice” in their Americanness, seeing such an appeal to be the most pragmatic move towards solidarity and social hope—one which is nevertheless held back by a mocking left who view America as forever unforgivable. He made enemies with many on the left when he stated, as a good leftist, that the academic left needed to “put a moratorium on theory” before it theorises itself into political irrelevance. Rorty knew that occupying such a middle position is notoriously dangerous and treacherous; it is to admit complexity and eschew absolute solutions; to mediate between and negotiate with the extremes of both ends. This 'both-siding' is invariably interpreted as weakness and cowardice, of having no firm principles to stand on, and its often accused of secretly occupying one end of the spectrum over another, since radicals from both sides can only see things in absolutes.

Yet this description does not do justice to Rorty’s complex views. He sought reform and accommodation within the invaluable and fragile (as exemplified by the recent Trump saga) liberal institutions, they sought a clean sweep, radical change, destruction, revolution, and new foundations that determinately follows the libretto of history. He sought pragmatism, solidarity, and the downplaying of differences, they sought amplification of differences in the name of diversity, anti-patriotism, and abstract theorising. To them there was nothing in between, to him, the in-between is where a self-critical and open minded individual who admits complexity finds herself.

But despite his contempt for blind faith in Utopian fantasies, whether Marxist or populist, his natural inclination remained to the left wing, to Popper's open society, the party of inclusivity and humanity. And his solemn enemies were always the right wing, those who are indifferent to poverty, to the plights of women, ethnic and sexual minorities and the marginalised. In an LRB article, Rorty [warned](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v16/n20/richard-rorty/a-leg-up-for-oliver-north) that

> the well-organised, well-financed and very energetic religious Right is a hundred times more threatening to free speech and genuine diversity of opinion than all the multiculturalists put together . . . A large portion of the American middle class has been brought to believe that the universities are under the control of a ‘political correctness’ police. This belief has made it possible for the racists, the sexists and the homophobes to dismiss criticism by sneering: ‘Gosh. You mean my political correctness indoctrination isn’t advanced enough?’. . . It is also true that the farthest-out 2 per cent [of university teachers] continue to write in a barely intelligible jargon. But compared to the ravings of the fundamentalist preachers about God’s hatred for gays and lesbians, such prattle is merely quaint.

Although he did not approve of political labels, in the final analysis I think Rorty roughly fits the description of a Rawlsian social democrat, one who is sympathetic to Marx's dreams but remains stubbornly anti-revolutionary and pro-reform.

The upshot of the \*Lectures\* is therefore an invitation to the left to get its priorities straight. The Left, Rorty argues, should have redistribution at the front of its agenda while doing its best to eliminate prejudice, but drop altogether the idea of 'cultural recognition'. The latter he construes as the idea that "cultures have value simply by virtue of being cultures"—a tradition of identity politics which exalts and calls for "recognition" of a disadvantaged groups' distinctive culture. This, to Rorty, not only divides people, muddles up the real issue (which is institutional and economic), and essentialises culture, but it also oppresses and imprisons those who are initiated into a culture by virtue of ascription, through forcing such individuals to conform to it. I do not think that these are conservative remarks and are in fact very much agreeable with progressivist thought.

## Did Rorty Miss the Point of Identity Politics?

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/382fc48c-dde1-4895-80a5-dea283264785_1200x675.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Kglh!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F382fc48c-dde1-4895-80a5-dea283264785_1200x675.jpeg)

One may rightly contend that Rorty’s framing of the American socio-political landscape ignores the fact that ‘American’ and ‘working class’ are themselves social identities and subject to 'identity politics', and so there is never an escape from identity politics, especially where identities are so salient in politics. Rorty, speaking as an educated, rich white man in a democratic, industrialist country, may not face the saliency of identity categorisation on political life and the precarity of some solely in virtue of their identities.

And his dismissal of race-consciousness in favour of class-consciousness and a colour-blind nationalist ideology a la Martin Luther King is a little outdated to modern Leftist ears insofar as it can be interpreted to assume a post-race world and has been accused of only serving to perpetuate systemic racism. The nostalgia Rorty longs for in the patriotic 19th century America of Dewey and Whitman was, at any rate, a racist America where slavery was still a norm and immigration was restricted only to white people.

A spectrum of the shades of human skin tones and human hair textures, human facial morphology and physique certainly does exist, there certainly *are* slight variations in human genes, there certainly *are* statistical frequencies in alleles within human population clusters, and these variations sometimes *do* correlate with societal stereotypes, selective injustice, disempowerment and so on, but carving up these slight variations into different categories and calling them ‘races’ only opens the path for that injustice to happen in the first place by re/producing a world of races. Race-talk allows the possibility for ‘races’ to be placed in superior-inferior hierarchies and for racial identity to be thought of as having intrinsic value. And so there is no utility gained from continuing to draw boundaries between these spectrums, just as there is no utility of drawing boundaries between different shades of rocks which can be subdivided into hundreds of millions of categories—the difference is that the former categorising game is not just pointless but also pernicious. If the concept ‘race’ is hierarchical by its nature and only encourages a naive and empirically untenable generalisation in which individuals are thought to “have” a race, then talk of race should be abjured.

There is a patriotic solidarity that permeated early 20th century America, and the endorsement of that attitude does not necessarily entail the endorsement of all actions carried out within that historic period. One may even argue that it is the socialisation of children into being proud heirs of a country that enshrines values of liberty and equality and a tradition of incremental progress that subsequently lead it to free its slaves, enfranchise its women, give equal rights to sexual minorities and institute trade unions.

On the other hand, an attitude towards America as irredeemably evil and beyond forgiveness, Rorty would argue, breeds indifference and divisions—precisely the image of many American citizens today. He would acknowledge that America’s past wrongs—the importation of African slaves, recurrent lynchings, the slaughtering of indigenous tribes, the oppression of women, the massacre of innocents in its foreign wars and so on—has an impact on individuals living in the present, so addressing those effects is important for its own sake, but in order to address them (Rorty for e.g., himself a supported of affirmative action) Americans must rejoice in the spirits of national solidarity and pride and view them through the lens that their country “remains unachieved” rather than “unachievable”. Self-hatred and disgust gets us no where. The view of America being asked of us to envisage is one of a long historical human project towards a classless, socially just and egalitarian community. Pride and hope for the future springs action and agency, self-loathing and a view that oneself or nation as being ontologically corrupt only encourages spectatorship and nihilism.

The first sort of attitude Rorty attributes to the principled Left—the party of hope, while the second is to be found in the current Left who, in practice, rejoin with the conservatives by keeping things as they are. While conservatives espouse American exceptionalism and chauvinism, the current “cultural” Left inverts it to the other extreme where America needs to be destroyed and built afresh from the ground up, a millenarianist aspiration that will never happen in practice or would, as history tells us, lead to great suffering and death.

Rorty puts this kind of objection nicely in his essay *[A Leg-Up for Oliver North](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v16/n20/richard-rorty/a-leg-up-for-oliver-north)*:

> . . . the simple, straightforward, vicious, terrifying racism which still ensures that most blacks and browns have to struggle desperately, and often hopelessly, for jobs, status and security is not a result of failure to ‘recognise cultural diversity’. As in the rest of the world, it is a way of ensuring that the descendants of those presently in power will always, automatically, have an advantage because of their easily recognisable, desirable, colour. Racism, here as elsewhere, increases as soon as jobs and opportunities decrease, and lessens as they increase. If racism ever ends in the US, it will be as a result of enduring affluence.

And one step towards mitigating the wealth disparity is “appealing, as Martin Luther King did, to the whites’ sense of justice to their fellow Americans”, instead of preaching that whites and blacks have separate cultural identities and not the same one, which would include the legacies of the Rosa Parks, the Fredrick Douglas’ and the MLKs as well as the Lincolns and the Jane Adams’ as part of its imagined mythos.

Of course not everyone would buy this rejoinder. Britain’s leading Marxist thinker, Terry Eagelton, wrote in an ironic [piece](https://socialistregister.com/index.php/srv/article/view/5575/2473), that he sees Rorty’s determination of appealing to the label “American”, instead of “human being”, (which Rorty says is intuitively and expediently more convincing basis for justice for black Americans) as being too parochial and implicates us in intolerance and cruelty. He adds:

> One of the benefits of trying to scupper a radical politics in this way is that it doesn't make you appear quite such a red-neck reactionary as you might otherwise look. You  can  seem to be talking about the ineluctability of belief systems or the fact that institutionality goes all the way down whereas in fact you are talking about  preserving the possibility of turning a fast buck.

I suspect the idea for Rorty was that rallying around a group that people can more closely identify with and share common memories about, instead of an abstract 'humanity', is more pragmatic as it rallies oppositions from all over the country, from the Left and the Right.

So much for Rorty's liberal patriotism.

Another, sharp critique came from Nancy Fraser, who argued that it's not clear that social democracy and redistributing the wealth are actually in conflict with multiculturalism and the recognition of differences; we can walk and chew gum at the same time. In reply to Rorty's stance against the politics of recognition, Fraser argued that Rorty's polemics against identity politics is sound to the extent to which Rorty characterises 'identity politics', but he is wrong in conflating the politics of recognition with that one characterisation of identity politics.

Recognition politics, Fraser says, should rather be construed in terms of *status* and not group identity because she takes misrecognition, that is, ignoring *status* differences, to entail social subordination and not being allowed to "participate as a peer in social life as a result of institutionalised patterns of cultural value". She notes:

> To redress the injustice requires a politics of recognition, but this does not mean identity politics [...] rather, it means a politics aimed at overcoming subordination *by deinstitutionalising patterns of cultural value that impede parity of participation and replacing them with patterns that foster it*. [...] Examples including marriage laws that exclude same-sex partnerships as illegitimate and perverse, social-welfare policies that stigmatise single mothers as sexually irresponsible scroungers, and policing practices such as racial profiling that associate racialised per- sons with criminality. By no means simple by-products of political economy, such instances of misrecognition cannot be redressed by a politics of redistribution alone. On the contrary, the only way to overcome the injustice is to replace institutionalised cultural patterns that subordinate people with patterns that establish them as peers. (2000: 23-24).

Rorty was therefore probably right about one thing and wrong about another. He was right to distrust a politics that treats cultures as bounded objects possessing intrinsic value or that mistakes the refinement of vocabularies for the reform of institutions. But it does not follow that redistribution exhausts the demands of equality. Some inequalities are reproduced through institutionalised meanings and status distinctions that have to be changed in their own right.
