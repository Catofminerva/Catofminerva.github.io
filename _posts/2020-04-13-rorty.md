### UPDATE 27/08/2026: 
This essay was first written in 2020, when I was around 23 years old. It was written during the height of what is now called 'Woke 1.0'. 'Woke 1.0' was great. Living through the 2010s felt like the crescendo of a 70-year linear march towards justice, and one is not be faulted for having had to believe in a libretto of history in which the 'moral circle' simply expands outward. In many respects, Woke 1.0 replaced discourses of sadism and cruelty and extended our imaginations of others that are alien to us and whom we have failed to notice. It also sometimes did well in replacing some outmoded liberal universal moral principles with an appeal to people’s sentimentality towards the particularities of marginalised groups; showing us how it *feels* to be in the shoes of a member of one of these groups, with the hope of expanding our sensibilities beyond our narrow experiences. We were made an altogether better society as a result, and people who were previously marginalised had, in many respects, led better, more richer, less humiliating lives. 

Some time in 2023, Woke 1.0 had reached its limit as a tool of persuasion and many of the gains it achieved had been fully reversed by a more hostile, conservative Overton window. I suspect that this is because one necessary prerequisite for the sensitivity that it demands from others is security; including material security. And in that respect, there had been very little progress and none to which the tools of Woke 1.0 had any viable solution. Gen Z and young millennials came to age materially disillusioned and their political focus naturally shifted downward on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs. For some, the 2010s cultural rhetoric of 'recognition' felt alienating in that regard, and many men in particular began seeking out conservative spaces that validated their economic and social anxieties. It became clear by that time that making 'cultural recognition' as a central goal for leftist politics was a big mistake. It could not bring into being state-backed housing insulation from the climate, strict corporate regulation, universal healthcare and baseline economic security.

In some other respects, Woke 1.0's demand for 'recognition' indeed made some of these marginalised groups lead worse lives. With recognition politics, for example, trans people were dragged from a state of relatively quiet, private existence into the blinding glare of the culture wars. Prior to this hyper-visibility, the general public largely subsumed them under a vague, passive universalism, a general sentiment that 'people should be left alone to live their lives' that was rooted in basic human decency. But Woke 1.0's demanded for absolute, granular cultural recognition and thereby inadvertently stripped away this protective obscurity. It transformed vulnerable individuals into abstract political symbols to be used as cannon fodder and an ultimate scapegoat for reactionary forces.

Rereading this essay again in 2026, it seems like many of Rorty's insights and warnings in the late 1990s had come true and it is hard not to make the parallels with the election of Trump. But there at least one respect in which I now disagree with Rorty and my former self. Where he looks unto moderate reform as a panacea for, I am not so sympathetic to his hostility towards the 'Revolutionary' left. Indeed I am more inclined today towards [anarchism](https://margaretkilljoy.substack.com/p/anarchism-and-its-misunderstanders) today than I am towards the middle-of-the-road leftist author you encounter here. 


## **The Old Reformist Left**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b6ca5bb9-b6b9-42e0-b5af-696675c0ba12_1973x1312.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5sgt!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb6ca5bb9-b6b9-42e0-b5af-696675c0ba12_1973x1312.jpeg)

*Achieving Our Country* is a series of lectures delivered by American Pragmatist philosopher Richard Rorty in 1997, in which he reflects on American Leftism in the past century. In one of the lectures, *American National Pride: Whitman and Dewey*, Rorty laments the state of the American Left of his time and its apparent abandonment of early Leftist ideals pertaining to hope and patriotism (the old-school, FDR New Deal, labor-union left), which, he says, have been substituted by excessive and abstract theorising, self-hatred, and resigned nihilism. He contends that the academic Left has turned its back on "real leftist politics" to indulge in esoteric cultural and identity pursuits from the comfort of their ivory towers, content with passively condemning "the system," "capitalism," "neoliberalism," and "neocolonialism," rather than proposing tangible, specific social policies to alleviate human suffering.

"The old reformist Left", we are informed, is a patriotic American Left inspired by the likes of John Dewey and Walt Whitman, espousing a rhetoric of commonality and solidarity rather the preoccupation with differences; with identifying unique intersectional struggles and demanding specific recognition. This old Left admits the constructive nature of national identity while nevertheless understanding its utility for solidarity and a basis from which to appeal to justice (“fellow Americans”). The old Left found it politically expedient to use passé clichés such as "I don't see colour; I only see you as an American", not as a way to hand-wave current injustices or elide the fact that such colour blindness does not currently exist, but as a way to appeal “as Martin Luther King did, to the whites’ sense of justice to their fellow Americans”, instead of preaching that whites and blacks have separate cultural identities and not the same, American, one. 

A lot of Rorty's beliefs on this, I suspect, is influenced by his philosophical work elsewhere. The idea is that, national identity *is* a myth; it is not meant to be a description of something waiting out there to be rationally discovered and dissected. “Reason,” writes Rorty, “can only follow paths that the imagination has first broken” (in this he echoes Dewey, who wrote that “Imagination is the chief instrument of the good"). We are made not one iota worse off by knowing that national identity is a mythical constructed. What remains to be asked is what kind of myth we want it to be and, by extension, what kind of people we want to become, since national narratives give us murky reminders and summaries of the considerations we care about, what sorts of things people like *us* do.

This is an extension of how norms are constituted in a society. Moral norms are often mistaken to be a process of discovering timeless 'moral facts' and then communicating them to those unfortunate people who have not yet discovered them, from which they would be persuaded just as easily as they would accept the proposition that 2 + 2 = 4. But reasons and justifications are not properties instantiated in the world in the form of ready-made principles, and then mapped on to our linguistic constructs; they rather accrue meaning within historically conditioned vocabularies and communities of justification. Moral disagreements are especially resistant since our moral beliefs are bound up with our loyalties, self-images and ideas about the sorts of people who we want to be like. 

On the Rortyan picture, then, when an individual asks herself ‘how should I act?’, this is implicitly indexed to another: who is my *we*? Much ordinary moral education already has this form: *we do not hit people; we do not treat people like that; we do not wear clothes like them*. Moral progress often consists in changing the scope or self-description of this *we* and bring people who had previously figured as strangers or inferiors within the community to whom our existing norms apply. National mythology in this sense is profoundly important for politics. “Fellow Americans” obviously is not an argument derived from universal moral premises but an invitation to enlarge an inherited moral identity, to redescribe the black American, the immigrant, the homosexual, or whoever had previously been excluded as one of *us*, and then to ask whether this is how we treat our own.

The American narrative, as imagined by Dewey and Whitman, includes the legacies of the Rosa Parks, the Fredrick Douglas’ and the MLKs as well as the Lincolns and the Jane Adams’ as part of its imagined mythos. It is grounded on an idea of human fraternity and in the human hope that the future will surpass the past. This is an America where people of diverse backgrounds come together to create a unified culture that transcends its individual components. 

Though there exists another, darker narrative of America, one founded on bloodshed and brutality, and Rorty concedes that both accounts are historically accurate, but he argues that dwelling on the latter with self-disgust is a vestige of the theological concept of 'sin.' Rorty argued that the Cultural Left became paralysed by "debilitating shame" over the country's past sins (slavery, imperialism, discrimination) and that "national pride is to countries what self-respect is to individuals: a necessary condition for self-improvement". 

To win elections in a conservative, nationalist era, future progressives will have to stop framing their countries as inherently evil projects, and instead tell inspiring stories about how expanding the moral circle is a deeply patriotic act. He suggests that we view these past heinous acts as failures of imagination by previous generations, who operated within a historically defined framework of justice. The slave trade, the massacre of Native Americans, and foreign wars exemplify this framework's shortcomings, as the America that institutionalised lynching failed to uphold its proclaimed ideals of liberty and equality.

Yet, Rorty posits that ‘America’ is a floating signifier, an open-ended project, a work in progress. While American forebears (previous participants in this project) failed to empathise with and walk in the shoes of disenfranchised women, slaves, Vietnamese children, and others, nevertheless condemning this ongoing endeavour as irredeemably wicked only leads to the stagnation of the project and its ideal. This mindset, prevalent today in what Rorty calls ‘the cultural left’, fosters a milieu of passive observation, theorising, and castigation, with the misguided hope that the project will one day be torn asunder, by a bloody revolution if need be, and start afresh with a blank page.

To Rorty, the ‘cultural left’ overlook that America is a project grounded on egalitarian ideals and and that this therefore calls for a patriotic narrative of national solidarity to instantiate it (in the March on Washington speech, MLK hoped that “one day this nation will rise up and live out the true meaning of its creed”). Rorty therefore calls on his fellow Americans to “rejoice” in their Americanness, seeing such an appeal to be the most pragmatic move towards solidarity and social hope—one which is nevertheless held back by a mocking left who view America as forever unforgivable. He made enemies with many on the left when he stated, as a good leftist, that the academic left needed to “put a moratorium on theory” before it theorises itself into political irrelevance.

The Cultural Left, Rorty says, espouses the idea that "cultures have value simply by virtue of being cultures"—a tradition of identity politics which exalts and calls for "recognition" of a disadvantaged groups' distinctive culture. This focus on differences, to Rorty, not only divides people, muddles up the real issue (which is institutional and economic), and essentialises culture, but it also oppresses and imprisons those who are initiated into a culture by virtue of ascription, through forcing such individuals to conform to it. This cultural left fail, in his view, in responding to the deeper ailments that agitate the working classes, those whose great suffocated cries are yet unheard by an 'identity-politics' obsessed Left. 

Rorty explicitly predicted that if the Left kept prioritising cultural recognition over economic security, the white-collar elite would enrich themselves while the working class was left behind. He warned that the working class would eventually realise that the democratic system wasn't working for them and would look for a "strongman to vote for", someone who would roll back social progress and implement economic protectionism. This prediction essentially foretold the mid-2010s to mid-2020s populist rightward shift and the Donald Trump election which saw America slip into a far-right populist government. Rorty observes:

> At that point, something will crack. The nonsuburban electorate will decide that the system has failed and start looking around for a strongman to vote for—someone willing to assure them that, once he is elected, the smug bureaucrats, tricky lawyers, overpaid bond salesmen, and postmodernist professors will no longer be calling the shots…[and] people will wonder why there was so little resistance to his evitable rise...where, they will ask, was the American Left? […]
>
> One thing that is likely to happen is that the gains made in the past fifty years by black and brown Americans, and by homosexuals will be wiped out. Jocular contempt for women will come back in fashion. The words “nigger” and “kike” will again be heard in the workplace. All the sadism which the academic Left tried to make unacceptable to its students will come flooding back. All the resentment which badly educated Americans feel about having manners dictated to them by college graduate will find an outlet (Rorty 1998:89-90).

## **What Kind of Leftist Was Rorty?**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/29cb35a9-bb3b-43c2-9f09-07d61464db80_1920x1280.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ArYZ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F29cb35a9-bb3b-43c2-9f09-07d61464db80_1920x1280.jpeg)

Rorty's appeal to Americanism is not a symptom of reactionary nationalism. He explicitly praised the Cultural Left for decreasing the amount of "sadism" and humiliation in daily life toward women and sexual minorities. His natural inclination remained to the left wing, to Popper's open society, the party of inclusivity and humanity. And his solemn enemies were always the right wing, those who are indifferent to poverty, to the plights of women, ethnic and sexual minorities and the marginalised. In an LRB article, Rorty [warned](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v16/n20/richard-rorty/a-leg-up-for-oliver-north) that

> the well-organised, well-financed and very energetic religious Right is a hundred times more threatening to free speech and genuine diversity of opinion than all the multiculturalists put together . . . A large portion of the American middle class has been brought to believe that the universities are under the control of a ‘political correctness’ police. This belief has made it possible for the racists, the sexists and the homophobes to dismiss criticism by sneering: ‘Gosh. You mean my political correctness indoctrination isn’t advanced enough?’. . . It is also true that the farthest-out 2 per cent [of university teachers] continue to write in a barely intelligible jargon. But compared to the ravings of the fundamentalist preachers about God’s hatred for gays and lesbians, such prattle is merely quaint.

On the other hand, Rorty had been a constant subject of critique by the Revolutionary Left. Although, he admired Marx and other political philosophers who are great in “helping us envisage a social democratic utopia”,  but, to him, they had no use in giving us “new political weapons or freeing us from old political illusions”. For Rorty this is a notorious liberal predicament which leaves one stuck between two 'extremes'. Rorty is a revolutionary leftist when he speaks of dreams and utopias, of a classless and casteless society, but he was also absolutely repelled to all that was abstract, theoretical, general, absolute, eternal, universal; and he was drawn to the concrete, sharp and achievable. Occupying such a middle position is notoriously dangerous and treacherous; it is to admit complexity and eschew absolute solutions; to mediate between and negotiate with the extremes of both ends. This is invariably interpreted as weakness and cowardice, of having no firm principles to stand on, and its often accused of secretly occupying one end of the spectrum over another, since radicals from both sides can only see things in absolutes.

Although the right liked to believe that there is a straight road leading from Marx to the gulag, Rorty did not share this belief, nor did he believe there to be any clear-cut precursors from one individual thinker to out-right totalitarianism, and certainly not Marx who championed the cause of the poor and the overworked under the arms of a despotic industrialism. While the right were content with calling Rorty a moral nihilist and dangerous relativist who is corrupting the minds of their children, he was never 'radical' enough for the Revolutionary left either, and this made him a particularly despised figure and lead him to reclaim the title ‘bourgeoise liberal’, partly out of spite.


## Did Rorty Miss the Point of Identity Politics?

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/382fc48c-dde1-4895-80a5-dea283264785_1200x675.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Kglh!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F382fc48c-dde1-4895-80a5-dea283264785_1200x675.jpeg)

One may rightly contend that Rorty’s framing of the American socio-political landscape ignores the fact that ‘American’ and ‘working class’ are themselves social identities and subject to 'identity politics', and so there is never an escape from identity politics, especially where identities are so salient in politics. Rorty, speaking as an educated, rich white man in a democratic, industrialist country, may not face the saliency of identity categorisation on political life and the precarity of some solely in virtue of their identities.

And his dismissal of race-consciousness in favour of class-consciousness and a colour-blind nationalist ideology a la Martin Luther King is a little outdated to modern Leftist ears insofar as it can be interpreted to assume a post-race world and has been accused of only serving to perpetuate systemic racism. The nostalgia Rorty longs for in the patriotic 19th century America of Dewey and Whitman was, at any rate, a racist America where slavery was still a norm and immigration was restricted only to white people.

A spectrum of the shades of human skin tones and human hair textures, human facial morphology and physique certainly does exist, there certainly *are* slight variations in human genes, there certainly *are* statistical frequencies in alleles within human population clusters, and these variations sometimes *do* correlate with societal stereotypes, selective injustice, disempowerment and so on, but carving up these slight variations into different categories and calling them ‘races’ only opens the path for that injustice to happen in the first place by re/producing a world of races. Race-talk allows the possibility for ‘races’ to be placed in superior-inferior hierarchies and for racial identity to be thought of as having intrinsic value. And so there is no utility gained from continuing to draw boundaries between these spectrums, just as there is no utility of drawing boundaries between different shades of rocks which can be subdivided into hundreds of millions of categories—the difference is that the former categorising game is not just pointless but also pernicious. If the concept ‘race’ is hierarchical by its nature and only encourages a naive and empirically untenable generalisation in which individuals are thought to “have” a race, then talk of race should be abjured.

There is a patriotic solidarity that permeated early 20th century America, and the endorsement of that attitude does not necessarily entail the endorsement of all actions carried out within that historic period. One may even argue that it is the socialisation of children into being proud heirs of a country that enshrines values of liberty and equality and a tradition of incremental progress that subsequently lead it to free its slaves, enfranchise its women, give equal rights to sexual minorities and institute trade unions.

On the other hand, an attitude towards America as irredeemably evil and beyond forgiveness, Rorty would argue, breeds indifference and divisions—precisely the image of many American citizens today. He would acknowledge that America’s past wrongs—the importation of African slaves, recurrent lynchings, the slaughtering of indigenous tribes, the oppression of women, the massacre of innocents in its foreign wars and so on—has an impact on individuals living in the present, so addressing those effects is important for its own sake, but in order to address them (Rorty for e.g., himself a supported of affirmative action) Americans must rejoice in the spirits of national solidarity and pride and view them through the lens that their country “remains unachieved” rather than “unachievable”. Self-hatred and disgust gets us no where. The view of America being asked of us to envisage is one of a long historical human project towards a classless, socially just and egalitarian community. Pride and hope for the future springs action and agency, self-loathing and a view that oneself or nation as being ontologically corrupt only encourages spectatorship and nihilism.

The first sort of attitude Rorty attributes to the principled Left—the party of hope, while the second is to be found in the current Left who, in practice, rejoin with the conservatives by keeping things as they are. While conservatives espouse American exceptionalism and chauvinism, the current “cultural” Left inverts it to the other extreme where America needs to be destroyed and built afresh from the ground up, a millenarianist aspiration that will never happen in practice or would, as history tells us, lead to great suffering and death.

Rorty puts this kind of objection nicely in his essay *[A Leg-Up for Oliver North](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v16/n20/richard-rorty/a-leg-up-for-oliver-north)*:

> . . . the simple, straightforward, vicious, terrifying racism which still ensures that most blacks and browns have to struggle desperately, and often hopelessly, for jobs, status and security is not a result of failure to ‘recognise cultural diversity’. As in the rest of the world, it is a way of ensuring that the descendants of those presently in power will always, automatically, have an advantage because of their easily recognisable, desirable, colour. Racism, here as elsewhere, increases as soon as jobs and opportunities decrease, and lessens as they increase. If racism ever ends in the US, it will be as a result of enduring affluence.

And one step towards mitigating the wealth disparity is “appealing, as Martin Luther King did, to the whites’ sense of justice to their fellow Americans”, instead of preaching that whites and blacks have separate cultural identities and not the same one, which would include the legacies of the Rosa Parks, the Fredrick Douglas’ and the MLKs as well as the Lincolns and the Jane Adams’ as part of its imagined mythos.

Of course not everyone would buy this rejoinder. Britain’s leading Marxist thinker, Terry Eagelton, wrote in an ironic [piece](https://socialistregister.com/index.php/srv/article/view/5575/2473), that he sees Rorty’s determination of appealing to the label “American”, instead of “human being”, (which Rorty says is intuitively and expediently more convincing basis for justice for black Americans) as being too parochial and implicates us in intolerance and cruelty. He adds:

> One of the benefits of trying to scupper a radical politics in this way is that it doesn't make you appear quite such a red-neck reactionary as you might otherwise look. You  can  seem to be talking about the ineluctability of belief systems or the fact that institutionality goes all the way down whereas in fact you are talking about  preserving the possibility of turning a fast buck.

I suspect the idea for Rorty was that rallying around a group that people can more closely identify with and share common memories about, instead of an abstract 'humanity', is more pragmatic as it rallies oppositions from all over the country, from the Left and the Right.

So much for Rorty's liberal patriotism.

Another, sharp critique came from Nancy Fraser, who argued that it's not clear that social democracy and redistributing the wealth are actually in conflict with multiculturalism and the recognition of differences; we can walk and chew gum at the same time. In reply to Rorty's stance against the politics of recognition, Fraser argued that Rorty's polemics against identity politics is sound to the extent to which Rorty characterises 'identity politics', but he is wrong in conflating the politics of recognition with that one characterisation of identity politics.

Recognition politics, Fraser says, should rather be construed in terms of *status* and not group identity because she takes misrecognition, that is, ignoring *status* differences, to entail social subordination and not being allowed to "participate as a peer in social life as a result of institutionalised patterns of cultural value". She notes:

> To redress the injustice requires a politics of recognition, but this does not mean identity politics [...] rather, it means a politics aimed at overcoming subordination *by deinstitutionalising patterns of cultural value that impede parity of participation and replacing them with patterns that foster it*. [...] Examples including marriage laws that exclude same-sex partnerships as illegitimate and perverse, social-welfare policies that stigmatise single mothers as sexually irresponsible scroungers, and policing practices such as racial profiling that associate racialised per- sons with criminality. By no means simple by-products of political economy, such instances of misrecognition cannot be redressed by a politics of redistribution alone. On the contrary, the only way to overcome the injustice is to replace institutionalised cultural patterns that subordinate people with patterns that establish them as peers. (2000: 23-24).

Rorty was therefore probably right about one thing and wrong about another. He was right to distrust a politics that treats cultures as bounded objects possessing intrinsic value or that mistakes the refinement of vocabularies for the reform of institutions. But it does not follow that redistribution exhausts the demands of equality. Some inequalities are reproduced through institutionalised meanings and status distinctions that have to be changed in their own right.
