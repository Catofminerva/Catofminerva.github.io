> “The Jews have divided into seventy-one sects, the Christians into seventy-two, and the Muslims shall be divided into seventy-three, the whole lot destined to Hell-fire except one [of each]” —Muslim Prophet Muhammad

The Wahhabis claim to be that one.

Two and a half centuries ago, they were a nascent movement, numbering no more than the Christian Apostles. The initiator of this movement, one Mohammed Ibn Abdul-Wahab (transliterally: the ‘the son of the servant of the Bestower’), along with a handful of followers, embarked on their mission in Najd, Central Arabia. During that time, most of the Arab population nominally identified as Muslims but there were many that remained as liberal, bountiful, pluralistic, pantheistic—not to say polytheistic, in their religious practices as anyone else. Some revered the graves of holy men, other pledged themselves to trees in the vicinity of these tombs, others called upon local demigods and spirits.

This resurgence of forms of ‘paganism’ mirrored the beliefs that the Islamic prophet Muhammad sought to eradicate. The Quran, by asserting the unity of a god, mandates worship, self-abnegation and supplication to one god alone. Armed with this idea and driven by the fervour of a reformer and the vigour of a revivalist, Ibn Abdul-Wahab endeavoured, through both words and sword, to steer the Arabs back to the unadulterated, monotone and standardised essence of Islam, as he took it to be, and its Unitarian doctrine.[^2]

## Idolatry, Paganism and Folk Religions in Arabia and the Greater Orient

> God as a king on high—our fathers, living under monarchy, rejoiced in that image and found it meaningful. His throne, his crown, his scepter, his seraphic retinue, his laws, rewards, and punishments.

—— Reverend Harry Emerson Fosdick (1926) 

The history of monotheistical faiths seems to me to adhere closely to a consistent pattern wherein an innate propensity draws men toward idolatrous veneration of a great many localised demigods, from which only by Divine Mercy are they able to overcome such inclinations, seek forgiveness for their transgressions, and continue their arduous spiritual journey to inherit the Kingdom of God in the comfortable palms of monotheism. The children of Israel, the Romans, and the Arabs all succumbed to this apparent predilection, and it is only through the guidance of sagacious figures, some divinely inspired, who rescue the tribe from the hideous jaws of polytheism and into blessed salvation. Ibn Abdulwahab is portrayed to be one such a figure.

Before I go into the particulars of Ibn Abdulwahab’s life, I'd like to first give the reader some broad account of Arab polytheism, however prodigiously difficult it is to discern the historical accuracy of any account of pre-modern Arabia. Following the advent of Islam, historical and philosophical research was subordinated to the study of the *hadith* (that is, the reported sayings and deeds of Muhammed) as the science par excellence. Intellectual currents were almost invariably Islamic through and through, and hence narrations of the past were seized and controlled by this dominant creed. Men were discouraged from studying the days of the *Jahiliya* (the pre-Islamic era), the name given to the pre-Islamic era itself translates into ‘the Age of Ignorance’, something only the cultural victors can afford to do. Thus pre-Islamic history was construed by Muslim historians[^3] in a cursory manner and much of the focus remained (and remains) on the post-Islamic periods.

But what do we know about the period immediately before Ibn AbdulWahhab? We again get the typical Manichean image of a 'period of ignorance'. Husayn ibn Ghannam concluded his chronicle of the burgeoning Wahhabi movement in the year 1810, and from his book has been drawn almost everything that Muslims believe they know of the religious condition of Arabia before the Wahhabi purgation, and contemporary Muslim scholars—who found that they first must invent a disease in order to sell the cure—extol Ibn Abdulwahab's aim of reviving the pristine faith by adhering to the traditions of the pious forebears and the teachings of the Qur'an and Hadith. Ibn Ghannam was nevertheless the typical partisan, a zealot writer, himself being a Wahhabi, and saw in Ibn Abdulwahab a marvellous reformist and described the time before him in what we are supposed to accept as unrelieved gloom. He writes that

> most Muslims had returned to the pre-Islamic darkness. Ignorant, at the mercy of potentates gone astray, deprived of the light of good guidance, they turned their backs on the book of God, thus imitating the custom of their ancestors. So they worshipped marabouts, living and dead, they venerated trees and substituted new idols for God. . . . Such was the situation in Najd . . . , in the holy places . . . in Yemen . . . , in Egypt . . . and in Iraq.

He describes the socio-cultural practices of the nomadic *Badu*, who lived a lifestyle radically detached from the urban and agricultural centres of the Ottoman Empire, and thought of their religion as a kind of austere monotheism of the open sky. They governed themselves by *Urf* (tribal customary law) rather than Islamic Sharia and "knew nothing of Islam except the Shahada (the testimony of faith)".

It is no doubt true that the Bedouins barely held on to an organised religion, otherwise the subsequent Ikhwan (The Brotherhood) movement would not make sense. Conversely, it was more acceptable practice among the townspeople to maintain their own sacred spaces which Ibn Ghannam describes, including *inter alia* venerated trees, stones and so forth, to a non-trivial extent thanks to the influence of Turkish Sufism. The traveller in the Hejaz and the Yemen and, where older usages have survived the reforming zeal of Nejd in the name of shirk, bidʿa or ṭāghū, will find again and again the marks of this practice in the grave of holy men, the *walī* (the 'friend' or protégé of God), covered by its *qubba*, and to which visitors of yesterday paid *ziyāra* and the expectations of supernatural aid that was thought to accompany it (thought to be a sanctuary to those whom the Muslims calls *jinn*).

### The Wonders of Polytheism

Ibn Ghannam's characterisation of his Arabia is a historical anti-model for his ideal-type religion. Notwithstanding any metaphysically substantive thesis about the 'truth' of the matte, what is so wrong about polytheism? What is wrong of an Arabia that is, as we are informed, populated with various metapersons? Saints, jinn, ancestors, places and other-than-human mediating agencies to whom particular powers were attributed (whether such worshippers of eighteenth-century Najd were secretly maintaining the cult of al-Lāt is orthogonal[^4]).

There is, to my mind, something rather beautiful about the indigenous folk religions of the age. A sort of world that exhibited a kind of sentient ecology of interacting ontologies in which humans were merely one part of; that is to say, a kind of animism (though the Arabs themselves distinguished carefully between the the *jinn* who are morally variable, and the *awliyāʾ* as exceptionally holy humans, between the rock that is inhabited and the rock that is merely inert). The fundamental difference between these meta-persons and Allah (a former demi-god among them) was that they were *immanent* i.e. co-present with humans and existed in the realm of human affairs, while the latter is *transcendent*, existing “out there”, in the *ghayb* (the Hidden), independent of human needs and desires. The one is a neighbour, the other a king on high. The one is cajoled, the other only obeyed as the Father, the Lawgiver, the Judge. The one is known, literal, plain, the other indefinite and unknowable.

There is a difference, then, in invoking a plurality of demigods each having a specific role in human affairs, serving specific human functions in human activities, which is much more intimately bound to the actual living human being and his practical concerns as opposed to the vast shadowy, capricious, moralistic, hierarchy-obsessed, metaphysically abstract, ineffable and incommunicable God who is said to have abandoned earth and is divorced from human needs and interests, solely concerned with being worshipped for His own sake and issuing salvations and damnations upon him and her indifferently. This sovereign patriarch standing over and above lowly human affairs and relations, loving nothing more than being obeyed willy nilly, is the god of the missionaries that the pre-Islamic Arabs, and indeed the pre-Wahhabi Arabs of Ibn Abdulwahab’s own century, held no real interest in.

Anthropologists have long observed the invocations of “metapersons” in folk religions to empower humans in all manners of daily social activities such as gardening, hunting, warfare, trading, lovemaking, curing illnesses, cooking food and so on. As renowned anthropologist Michael Sahlins (2022) very recently noted “this engagement of metahuman power is an all-around cultural praxis: meaning, the implication of spiritual powers is the condition of the possibility of human social activities of every kind”.

Even the deities really were distinctly human (sometimes female, and sometimes, as I have noted in the case of ʿAthtar, of a curiously indeterminate or even doubled sex); served distinct human desires, interests and needs, such as the need to exploit nature, and consequently their inventiveness were infinitely more imaginative and superior than the absolutist and authoritative words vouchsafed in a sacred text of supernatural origin or through the medium of other, ostensibly wiser, men, who themselves held their own independent or theological or otherworldly interests. 

I say “otherworldly” because folk tribes did not ruminate too much about another world of a different constitution precisely because spirits—be they corporeal or immaterial, for after all humans too were considered spirits with an incorporeal soul, merely of a different kind—were in the final analysis terrestrial, earthly and participated in human life. Thus they saw no need in invoking a distinction between the natural and supernatural, and this too is not too unlike the fact that ‘we’ moderns talk about institutions, states, monetary systems as reified agents, and of corporations as persons with legal rights, abstractions walking among us. I have often reflected that the London stockbroker who speaks of 'the Market' as a sentient and capricious being is closer to the old Nabataean than he would care to admit. 

And though they often believed, when they cared to believe, in the immortality of the soul, this does not mean the existence of the individual as such, as the continuation of identity post-death in the manner of the Christian or the Muslim paradise, but rather it is the existence of the ‘soul’ itself, a portion of vitality which is not extinguished upon the grave, but which may be reincarnated as a human being or another embodied spirit.

In Heideggerian terms folk peoples epistemological credo was that *Being is being there*; that is to say, far from being ‘mere superstitious’ thinkers, they were proto-empiricists and their gods did not exist in a different realm of existence but were dependent for their ontology on irreducibly human experience. God(s) then were an 'event' that happens and not an abstracted hypothesis.  Or, to put the matter somewhat crudely, because humans are not self-sufficient; spirits of the dead, ancestors, and demigods were unexplained causal agents who played the role of invisible peers who helped in myriad daily functions, not least in controlling and predicting nature.

I am reminded here of the words of the anthropologist Stanley Walens, who has lived among the coastal peoples of the Pacific Northwest and who has written of their spirit-cults. As he astutely puts it, indigenous demigods were 

> “as practical and mechanical as techniques of wood-carving or fishing . . . No matter how skilled a woodcarver is, his carving will be excellent only insofar as he is helped by the spirits and their power to carve; the fisher-man, no matter how carefully he has constructed his weir, is doomed to failure unless he can invoke spirit-power to make the salmon come to the place where he is fishing” (Walens 1981).

Clearly these immanent spirits and demigods were less capricious than the transcendent God because they are, by their nature, invariably doing exactly what those who call upon them are also doing; their agency is reducible to human agency and experience, and divinity is a mere mirror of what actually instantiates on the ground: the land is more or less fertile, the crop grows or does not, the fish are caught or are not, women bear healthy children or they do not etc. The god is not looking below from above the process as opposed to being him or herself *the* process.

On a completely other note; as merely an aesthetic argument, I am perpetually struck with admiration for the inventive genius displayed in the conception of these demigods; in the elegance of their design and the sophistication of their refinement, they vastly surpass any supreme godhead. Finally, I must note that these demigods and their associated pre-islamic traditions never fully left Islam. Islamic tenet, "there is no God but God, and Mohammed is His apostle" was never entirely embraced by the Arab populace in its purest form. The ancient deities, such as Allat, Al Uzah, and Al Manat, whom Mohammed himself initially referred to as "the exalted women, whose intercession is hoped for" during his first recitation of the Quranic chapter An-Najm, were subsequently renounced, and the verses were later conveniently, in typical post-hoc rationalisation, alleged by Mohammad as having been ‘[satanic verses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satanic_Verses#Basic_narrative)’ mistaken for divine revelation.  (A once accepted incident which had later undergone historical revisionism and rendered fabricated to fit the orthodoxy of an infallible prophet). These, along with a multitude of lesser local demigods, subsequently relinquished their place to Allah, the paramount deity and sole remnant of the Meccan pantheon.

However, the lingering influence of these bygone gods persisted even though some tribes eventually shifted their devotion from their individual local deities to the veneration of a single patron saint. The ingrained local customs and rites were so deeply interwoven into the societal fabric that even Mohammed felt obliged to largely maintain a large part of the status quo, reluctant not to throw the baby with the bath water and merely prohibiting a handful of the most egregious violations. Hence practices such as the Hajj pilgrimages’ visitation of the sacred mountains of *Safa* and *Merwa* who were thought of previously as two cherished idols. Hence, too, the pre-Islamic tradition where *Quraysh* (the dominant tribe during Muhammed’s time) chanted the following verses as they circumambulated the Kaaba:

> By al-Lat and al-'Uzza,
> And Manat, the third idol besides.
> Verily they are the most exalted females
> Whose intercession is to be sought.

Hence, too, the tradition of hurling stones at the devil in the vale of Mina, including sacrificial elements the sacrifices of animals on the vale, the vigil (*al-waquf*) on *Arafah* and *Al-Muzdalafah*,  and the festivities on the new moon. These were all pre-Islamic pagan rituals and which presently remain largely unaltered as the iconoclastic prophet did not wish to volunteer too much in disintegrating, having been [quoted](https://answering-islam.org/Books/Al-Kalbi/uzza.htm) as saying "I have offered a white sheep to al-'Uzza, while I was a follower of the religion of my people" according to ibn al-Kalbi. One of Muhammed’s uncles and arch nemesis was pejoratively given him the *kunya* ‘Abu Lahab’, when his actual name is Abd Al-‘Uzzah (slave of Al-‘Uzzah demigoddess). The author of the Quran, the Lord of the Cosmos, devoted an entire, rather petty, chapter gushing and cooing over Abd Al-‘Uzzah’s forthcoming eternal burning in hellfire, for the most trivial of reasons[^5].

Though it scarcely ever occurred to the regional Arabs to stop prostrating themselves to something nonhuman altogether (with the exception of lone avant-garde, more recent, figures in the post-islamic era such as al-Ma'arri) and focus on their commitment to one another, what was nonetheless common among many of both the nomadic and settled is that Allah was one more deity among the pantheon of demigods. Hence Mohammed’s fathers’ name ‘Abdullah’ ‘the slave of Allah’ and where many others were named abdul-[name of a god], and also the Quranic verse: “And most of them believe not in God, without also associating other deities with Him“, Sûrah XII :106. 

Even if he may be more powerful, possessed a muscular development far exceeding that of his fellow deities, so to speak, he was full of abstract—and therefore useless when indexed to human practical needs—attributes; all in all, omnipotence, holiness, absoluteness, infinity, omniscience and the like. Pragmatist philosopher William James (1902) describes this problem with mono, omni-gods in his *Varieties*:

> Theism, whenever it has erected itself into a systematic philosophy of the universe, has shown a reluctance to let God be anything less than All-in-All. In other words, philosophic theism has always shown a tendency to become pantheistic and monistic, and to consider the world as one unit of absolute fact; and this has been at variance with popular or practical theism, which latter has ever been more or less frankly pluralistic, not to say polytheistic, and shown itself perfectly well satisfied with a universe composed of many original principles, provided we be only allowed to believe that the divine principle remains supreme, and that the others are subordinate.

The existence of evil in the world is a common problem for monotheistic religions that posit an all-powerful, all-knowing, and benevolent deity. Since everything must have its foundation in God, the existence of evil becomes harder to reconcile on this vision of an all loving monistic entity that is ultimately responsible for all actual and logically possible affairs, including evil ones. On the other hand, the existence of multiple demi-gods with varying powers and intentions dissolves the problem of the presence of evil, as not all gods are assumed to be all good or all powerful.

And so I maintain, against the whole tribe of missionaries and Wahhabi apologists, that the religion of pre-Islamic and pre-Wahhabi Arabia was not a degradation of an earlier monotheism but a distinct and, in its own terms, a perfectly coherent mode of being in the world. It was immanent, practical, plural, and exquisitely attentive to the grain of human experience. It was part of an unfathomably complicated society who had invoked meta-persons with various functions and jurisdictions, a society far more complex than the term *Jāhiliyya* would suggest. It was destroyed by Ibn Abdulwahab and his followers because it left no room for the *beyond*-human, the sovereign patriarch whom the reformers wished to install in the empty sky and upon which we must all prostrate ourselves. Lastly, olytheism also avoids some difficulties generated by omni-theism because no single deity must bear responsibility for every feature of the world.

## Monotheistic Puritanism

Ibn Abdulwahab ultimately came to the belief that there are fundamental, ‘essences’ of Islam that must be distinguished from its more recent or contingent accretions, eventually returning to what he considered the original viewpoint and basis of the Prophet and his initial disciples in Hejaz twelve centuries prior. To the son of Abdul Wahhab is attributed the apparent rediscovering of the long-lost keystone amid the wreckage of the Islamic edifice, and, even more daring and daunting, of possessing the temerity to conceive a scheme to restore it by employing it to rebuild the fragmented structure bottom up from whence it was.

This central premise from which all else logically follows is embodied in the oft-recited, habitually misapprehended, maxim, "La ilaha illa Allah", that is, "there is no god but God", a maxim that is a product of a history associated with the Meccan pantheon of deities described earlier. Not al Lat nor al Uzza, nor the many other demigods—but simply One demi-god, indeed now shedding the demi- and raised to the place of God, the One. Hence the incessant relation of the maxim, a reminder to the speaker that the gods of Mecca are naught, and the acclamation of the name of the monistic deity (*talbiya*) during the Hajj pilgrim, chanting

> Here we are O Lord! Here we are! Here we are!

> Thou hast no associate save one who is thine.

> Thou hast dominion over him and over what he possessed!.

Declaring his unity at a time in which this unity was rather precarious and not fully established among the Arabs.

While this is a verbatim translation, it is inadequate in conveying the complete import and influence of the Arabic phrase as it reverberates within the soul of the Wahhabi. Though "There is no god but God" in English seems to merely negate the presence of all divinity save for the one, the expression is much more elaborate than this transliteration. It stands for the absolute oneness of the Supreme Being who devoid of any multiplicity in essence or persona, and the idea that that this singular, exalted entity is the sole operative force, the lone agent, the unique origin of action throughout the cosmos. As a result, all other entities whether corporeal or ethereal, innate or sentient, physical or ethical, are reduced to a condition of pure, unadulterated passivity, irrespective of motion or quiescence, action or possibility; and the only might, movement, vigour and activity belong to God alone, while everything else from the most exalted archangel to the humblest amoeba and the atom, epitomises torpor and sheer instrumentality.

Thus, within the mere solitary expression "La ilaha illa Allah" we discern the quintessence of a belief system that is the ultimate self-abnegation to the non-human and in which all is subsumed within the despotic will of the singular, all-embracing Agent, who is moreover, fervently jealous of others being worshiped over Him. As a result of this jealousy, He is more predisposed to chastise than to bestow, to inflict pain rather than grant delight, and to demolish rather than construct. His singular gratification is in perpetually reminding created beings that they are naught but His slaves, subjects and tools—contemptible ones at that—so they may better acknowledge His supremacy and comprehend that His might, guile, will, and arrogance exceed their own—or rather, that there exists no power, guile, will, or arrogance apart from His.

Naturally, then, what was central to Ibn Abdulwahab's puritanism was the notion of *tawhid*, or the absolute oneness of God (Lacroix, 2011). It was as important to him as it was to his prophet before him to jettison the apparently indelible (and ironically immortal) Meccan pantheon from the consciousness of the succeeding generations of Arabs. He fervently believed that the veneration of saints, tomb visitation and the intercession of religious figures constituted acts of *shirk*, i.e., associating partners with God, and thus violated the cardinal principle of *tawhid* and the principal sin in Islam. His most known texts include *The Book of Monotheism*, *Clarification of Doubts* and *The Three Principles*. In the former, Ibn Abdulwahab writes:

> Know that the most egregious sin is to associate partners with Allah. It is the only sin that Allah does not forgive, for He says, 'Indeed, Allah does not forgive association with Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills' [Quran 4:48] (Ibn Abdulwahab, 2003).

Thus murder, torture and the like may be forgiven, but the notion that there are other gods is absolutely repelled by this envious One. This unwavering adherence to *tawhid* guided Ibn Abdulwahab's puritanical vision, shaping his views on a host of other religious practices, as when he vehemently opposed the use of charms and talismans, considering them to be tantamount to idolatry.

In accordance with this narrative as championed and instructed by the Wahabbi school of thought, predestination (or rather predamnation) is an organising principle. When God resolved to create humankind, He procured a lump of earth, clay, from which all people were to originate and wherein they, in a manner of speaking, 'pre-existed' in another form (thus the verse “We created man from sounding clay moulded from black mud”). Haphazardly dividing the matter into two parts, He hurled one half into the inferno and cast the other half into heaven. God consigns one individual to eternal suffering in fetters and seas of scorching fire while another is endowed with the felicity of an everlasting celestial palace on high and accompanied by forty heavenly beauties. Both ends are, all things considered, determined solely by His own volition and because He desires it. Above all, the principle endures: "La Ilah illa Allah", "there is no god but God".

Subsequent doctrines—which introduced notions of merit, human interdependence, intercessors, mediators, sacred sites, revered saints, upon which sepulchres had founded various ascetic fraternities—were henceforth considered damnable contaminations and perversions of the grand and unadorned vision of one absolute sovereign presiding over a uniform multitude of undifferentiated and indistinct serfs. The Wahhabi reformist deduced that the demarcation, which was always blurry, ought to be drawn at the existence of Muhammad and those who, by virtue of their intimate personal affiliation, are regarded as one with the Prophet himself. The era of the *Sahhabah* was thus arbitrarily taken to be the utmost boundary of what Islam ought to acknowledge and incorporate; anything beyond this is incongruous and adverse. Ibn Abulwahhab devoted the remainder of his days to restoring this pristine form of Islam, the Islam of Muhammad, the *Sahhabah*, and now his own, persuaded that this was the sole authentic, infallible, divinely disclosed path, and all else constituted mere human augmentation; and hence what must be done is to rewind the chronology of Islam to its pure genesis, which, in a manner, Islam was purposed to remain unaltered from the outset (else be faced with the problem in which the teachings of Muhammed, the *Suna*, comes to be historically contingent and relative). In its essence this notion demanded that Islam be, indeed is, static and persist as such such that anything that exhibits dynamism and motion is, by such a fact, indicted of heterodoxy and apostasy.

I do not wish to commentate too much on my own views but merely to maintain, with Palgrave, that life presupposes movement and maturation, both of which entail transformation; that it is unreasonable to reproach a living being for growing and metamorphosing; and that endeavouring to hinder it from doing so by confining it to a textual denomination or coercing it into a Puritarian framework is essentially tantamount to extinguishing it entirely. Stasis is Wahhabisms’ guiding precept and a fundamental necessity; thus, the son of 'Abdul Wahhab, in striving to revert Islam to its original simplicity and converting its inception into its ultimate aim, was to a certain extent accurate and indeed exhibited a profound comprehension of the nature and foundational notions of his faith as understood from a literal interpretation of the founding texts.

It is nevertheless essential to acknowledge that Ibn Abdulwahab's teachings were not entirely original and that he, rather adeptly, synthesised and encapsulated the works of preceding scholars, such as Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, and substantially drew upon, almost to a degree of plagiarism, the work of the Yemeni Muhammad ibn Isma'il San'ani, titled *Purification of the Doctrine from the Filth of Unbelief*. As San'ani similarly had journeyed through regions of the Arabian Peninsula, encompassing Yemen, Tihama, and the Hijaz, he observed practices that, in his estimation, contradicted the tenets of his Islam. San'ani was particularly troubled by the widespread cult of the dead and the veneration of living charlatans professing expertise in occultism and mysticism. Consequently, he wrote his seminal work on these matters and called for a return to the purity of Islamic doctrine. Thus one finds in this text paragraphs that Ibn Abdul Wahhab might as well had written himself:

> From this you have all learned that if someone believes that any of a tree, stone, grave, angel, jinni, the living and the dead, can help or harm, or approach God, or intercede with Him for mundane needs . . . they thus associated things to God and believed in what it is not allowed to believe, as polytheists (mushrikūn) believe in idols. Not to speak of one who makes a vow upon his property or son to the dead or the living, or demands of them what can be only demanded from the Exalted God. This is indeed exactly the same polytheism (shirk) of the idolaters (ʿubbād al-as.nām). Making vows to the dead with property, making sacrifices on graves, seeking help from the dead, and asking favours from them is exactly the same polytheism as in the pre-Islamic period of barbarism (jāhilīya).

And though Wahhabi scholars draw on al-San'ani to legitimise Ibn Al-Wahhab’s doctrines, yet, as per the account of Michael Cook, al-San'ani scrutinised several of Ibn Abdul Wahhab’s works in 1756 and deduced that the Shaykh was a poorly educated and naive individual who had not sought guidance from learned scholars such as himself. Instead, Wahhab had studied the works of Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya and dogmatically adopted their views in a simplistic and flawed manner. Al-San'ani withdrew his support for the destruction of graves a year later upon learning of the Wahhabis' rampant excommunication (*takfir*) of fellow Muslims, including the Zaydis, and the brutality they unleashed during their expansionist campaigns. It is said that he composed a poem condemning Ibn Abul Wahhab for the bloodshed caused by those who revered the dead in their graves.

## Return to Najd and the Rise of Wahhabism

Brimming with zeal for his undertaking and resolute in seeing it to fruition, Ibn Abdulwahab made his return to his native land of Najd after an absence of six years, a significant portion of which he had spent in Damascus. During this time, Central Arabia was fragmented among numerous chieftains, the most formidable being *Ebn-Ma'ammer* in *'Eyanah* in *Wadi Haneefah* and *Da'as* in the consequential district of *Manfoohah* in *Kharj*. Nevertheless, these potentates and their rivals were broadly under the dominion of *Ebn-Muflik*, who succeeded the Carmathian princes in Kateef and Hasa.

When he arrived, Ibn Abdul Wahhab found himself increasingly disquieted by what he deemed a lamentable departure from the true essence of Islam. He believed that nearly every trace of Islam had vanished from Najd. The adoration of the *Djann*, carried out beneath towering trees or within the recesses of *Djebel Toweyk*, was amalgamated with supplications to the deceased and offerings presented at their sepulchres. This amalgam was infused with remnants of archaic *Saban* superstitions, and even discernible influences from the doctrines of Moseylemah (who is condemned by name by the author of the Quran and given the *kunya* by Muhammed *al-kathab*, viz. ‘the liar’) and *Kermoot*—all pagan rituals which the puritan Ibn Abdulwahab thought was incongruous to the essence of Islam. Practices such as the adoration of saints, pilgrimages to tombs, and the intercession of spiritual luminaries were, in his lights, tantamount to idolatry and polytheism.

The Quran was rarely perused in Najd of the time, the quintet of daily prayers had been forsaken, and the direction of Mecca held no significance whatever. Tithes, ablutions, and pilgrimages were now unheard of notions, and the political and religious state of the territory was in disheveled disarray. Convinced that these practices were anathema to the purity of the faith, Ibn Abdulwahab resolved to resurrect what he took to be the original form of Islam by advocating for unyielding adherence to the sacred teachings of the Quran and Sunnah as supposedly practiced by men and women living during the 7th century CE, whilst repudiating all manner of religious innovation, or *bid'ah*.

Ibn Abdulwahab departed his place of birth, Uyayna, and resettled in the more prominent town of *'Eyanah*, under the aegis of its ruler, Ebn-Ma'ammer. Myriad and hyperbolised tales circulated within Najd regarding the erstwhile splendour of *'Eyanah* and the despotism of its chieftains. Ibn Abdulwahab’s ardor was intense, yet not impetuous, for he understood the necessity of patiently and prudently awaiting the incremental manifestation of his grand schemes. For a period, he resided in a house near the marketplace upon which he lead a tranquil and nearly secluded existence and abstained from preaching or striving to differentiate himself from the masses. Though at this particular time in our narrative, the people of *‘Eyanah*, who were deemed utter infidels by Ibn Abdulwahab, began to be gradually acquainted with him and  esteemed him for his knowledge, including Ebn-Ma'ammer. In due course, Ibn Abdulwahab discerned that the moment had arrived to pursue his foremost ambitious design, and not very long, an occasion presented itself in which he was able to make himself obnoxious to the city dwellers. One evening, as he reclined upon the rooftop of his home, overlooking the marketplace, a man who had misplaced a camel ambled past, vociferously invoking *Sa'ad*, the local demigod or patron saint of the city (which is also, by the way, a popular Arabic given male name literally meaning ‘happiness’), to aid him in locating the lost beast. "Why not implore the God of Sa'ad instead?" the Wahhabi proclaimed, with sufficient volume for all those in the market and street to overhear. This extraordinary expression, ostensibly unspoken for centuries in 'Eyanah, was said to incite both anger and curiosity, which in turn flung wide open a door for deliberations and the taking into pieces of prevalent ostensible blasphemies, to which the Wahhabi began to preach more openly. Soon enough the inhabitants of 'Eyanah found themselves divided into two factions: one advocating for Sa'ad and the other championing Allah. The disciples of the new instructor multiplied in both quantity and zeal with each passing day.

Ibn Abdulwahab's preaching would swiftly garner him the reverence and admiration customarily accorded to petty chiefs. He was said to have struck a formidable chord in the hearts of those who lived in *‘Eyanah*, a holy fear inspired by the potency of his pen which possessed the rare grace of being wondrously plain and simple to the unlettered laity.  The sense of trepidation that pervaded the era owed its existence to the vehement, unyielding, and obstinate dogmatism with which Ibn Abdulwahab sought to dismantle the doctrines he deemed unworthy and thus instilling a sense of dread in the hearts and minds of those who opposed him.

The act of tearing down tombs in conjunction with the toppling of hallowed trees and the infrequent practice of stoning adulteresses, became emblematic of the Wahhabi movement's character ever since. A particular grave, located in al-Jubayl within Wadi Hanifa, was allegedly desecrated by Ibn Abdulwahab himself. This tomb was believed to be the resting place of Zayd ibn al-Khattab, brother to the second caliph, 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, and a companion of Muhammad. This revered site was frequented by numerous devotees who offered prayers to Zayd, and its selection for destruction by Ibn Abdulwahab was calculated. It has been reported that the denizens of the oasis intended to protect their sacred space, but upon witnessing Ibn Abdulwahab accompanied by a force of 600 men, they stepped aside and observed the dismantling of their most cherished and beloved site.

Ebn-Ma'ammer maintained an air of nonchalance concerning the religious discord in the town. Nonetheless, several distinguished townsfolk, more attached to their ancient beliefs and customs, perceived the gravity of the situation. After multiple fruitless endeavours to rouse Ebn-Ma'ammer from his inertia, they apprised the supreme governor at Kateef, Ebn-Muflik, of the burgeoning puritanical doctrine's rapid proliferation and the local chieftain's negligence. They further cautioned him about the perils of Islam's rather ultramontane revival, not solely in 'Eyanah but throughout Najd.

As a Carmathian, Ebn-Muflik naturally harboured animosity towards Muslims. He promptly dispatched instructions to Ebn-Ma'ammer to cease the preaching and apprehend the preacher, menacing dire consequences if he persisted in supporting the Wahhabi. Rather than fully complying, an action that might have potentially snuffed out the nascent movement, Ebn-Ma'ammer adopted a lukewarm approach. He informed Ibn Abdulwahab that he could no longer safeguard him and counselled him to depart the vicinity to avert capture and any further ramifications. In short, we may owe the Wahhabi movement to the sheepishness and feebleness of Ebn-Ma’ammer.

## The Saudi Coalition and the Inheritance of Ibn Abdulwahab in the Gulf

*Mohammed Ibn Saud, first Saudi Monarch (I do not believe this is an actual photograph).*

This seeming reversal of fortune was a pivotal moment for the Wahhabi prospects, Ibn Abdulwahab was very quick to exploit it to his benefit. A mere six leagues from 'Eyinah, in a separate branch of Wadi Haneefah, was the stronghold of Derey'eeyah, fated for both grand renown and a woeful downfall. Here, amidst a small yet resolute populace, resided Mohammed Ibn Saud, the ruler of the district. Mohammed Ibn Saud was brave and enjoyed the loyal support of his relatives and followers.

Positioned between Ebn-Ma'ammer to the north and Dais to the southeast, Mohammed Ibn Saud found himself the object of envy from both sides, and his principality faced significant danger from these formidable and antagonistic neighbours. Ibn Abdulwahab arrived at Mohammed Ibn Saud's stronghold in 1744, seeking sanctuary, which an Arab chieftain rarely denies to a refugee and supplicant. However, their roles soon reversed as Mohammed Ibn Saud discerned the potential in the preacher and became a convert to his creed and Ibn Abdulwahab's daughter eventually went on to marry his son, Abdulaziz.

This was made all the more easier by yet another historical contingency; the help of a woman, one Mudha, the spouse of Amir Mohammed Ibn Saud, who interceded on behalf of Ibn Abdulwahab and played a crucial role in the ultimate success of the Wahhabi movement. Upon learning of the reformer and his mission, divine love for Ibn Abdulwahab was instilled in the heart of Mudha. Simultaneously, Amir Mohammed Ibn Saud was deeply enamoured with Mudha, who was likely his favoured wife. Heeding her counsel, she said, "This man has been sent to you by Allah; offer him your protection and form an alliance with him." Consequently, Amir Mohammed Ibn Saud complied with her request by providing Ibn Abdulwahab the safety and support he needed.

Trusting the ruler's ardent spirit, Abdulwahab divulged a plan with extensive implications for both this world and the next. He expounded on the plan's character and resources and concluded by asking the Chief to pledge him his word to make the cause of God his cause, and the sword of Islam his sword, and in return, Ibn Abdulwahab will position in him as the sole monarch of all of Najd and the first potentate in Arabia, to which Mohammed Ibn Saud consented. He adopted Islam in its most unadulterated form under the tutelage of the Wahhabi, selecting him as his exclusive advisor and mentor.

The Saudi polity expanded substantially across Najd under Mohammed Ibn Saud and his successors in which its progress was readily facilitated by the decline and collapse of 'Eyanah by internal upheavals as a result of various forms of misgovernment and despotism. Ibn Abdulwahab dedicated his waning years to the edification of Derey'eeyah and provide succour to the Sauds' martial pursuits through his fervent religious guide. I have perused many of his scholarly works, which predominantly elucidate and substantiate the principles peculiar to his religious following, and concluded that such pitiful writings, which may be regarded with admiration by a 16th-century Radical Iconoclast or a Scottish Covenanter or even be confused with the edicts of Calvin’s *Genevan Consistory* by a strict legalist, are the ravings of a pedant who, by hair-splitting theological trifles within the high mysteries of a jurisprudence already riddled with contradictions and metaphysical anomalies—matters at any rate wholly foreign to the common mind—seeks only to cloal political ambition and martial conquest in the name of righteous law.

As it happened, few of his contemporaries had the opportunity to cross paths with the man himself, given that the most significant years of his life were devoted to serving the later monarch of Najd and attending to his court where he held the esteemed position of exclusive advisor. Ibn Al Wahhab exhibited an unwavering earnestness in his devotions and refrained from wielding political influence or asserting any entitlement to meddle in matters of the state, left contented with his station as a spiritual guide and basking in the esteem of those in his proximity until his days drew to a close. His mortal remains were interred in Derey'eeyah, following the demise of his benefactor Mohammed Ibn Saud, and he left in his wake a lineage of sons with a multitude of reported appellations. The scions of the Ibn Wahhab line have consistently occupied the most exalted positions in the judiciary and the religious hierarchy of the Wahhabi realm, in Saudi Arabia, amassing considerable fortunes along the way.

It has been chronicled that in the course of his life, Ibn Abdulwahab entered into matrimonial unions with several women and siring numerous progeny (Lacroix, 2011). It is said that his marriages were marked by an atmosphere of piety, discipline, and a shared commitment to the austere principles he espoused. The puritanical views of Ibn Abdulwahab's youth would become entrenched in the peninsula and emerge as the bedrock of what many now regard as the ‘true Islam’ (Al-Rasheed, 2010). When the concept of religion is separated from the potential to cause humiliation and suffering to those who do not adhere to the "right" beliefs, it becomes less appealing to a significant number of individuals. The appeal further diminishes for even more people when the assurance of reuniting with our cherished ones after passing away is also removed from the equation. In stark contrast to the more liberal interpretation that suffused the milieu of his era, the austere doctrines Ibn Abdulwahab espoused would come to be accepted as a return to the faith's origins, the one and true interpretation, indelibly altering the course of Islamic history.

From such a moment, three centuries ago, until today, the enduring alliance between the missionary endeavours of the Wahhabis and the martial prowess of the Sauds has persisted, a partnership between religious fervour and sheer military and political power.  After several further decades of conflict, Mohammed Ibn Saud's grandson, Saud the Great, extended Saudi rule over much of the Arabian Peninsula, including the two holy cities of Mecca and Medina, thereby consolidating the Unitarian faith across the region. Wahhabism spread widely into the Arabian peninsula and the Wahhabis of the 19th and early 20th century were especially distin­guished by a puritanical self-abasement and self-mortification, and a stern opposition to the pleasures of the world and especially tobacco, silk and wine.

Ibn Abdulwahab's puritanical doctrine, once the animating force of a singular man's vision, has indelibly become our contemporary Gulf’s dogmatic creed. The austere interpretation of Islam he championed has been institutionalised in sundry aspects of the nation's religious, educational, and legal spheres. In the final analysis, this ominous precursor of present-day reactionaries was a staunch absolutist, an unyielding theocrat, and a resolute legitimist, worshipping a grotesque unity that placed God at the apex and the lowly human bellow. He was a tireless advocate for the most severe, narrow, and inflexible dogmatism, a shadowy figure hailing from the Dark Ages, part theocrat, part inquisitor, and part monarchist. His Islam was characterised by terror, passive obedience, and a religion subsumed by the state; his God thrived solely through the sword of the burgeoning monarch. Arrogant, bigoted, and unwavering, he nevertheless possessed a formidable will and an uncanny ability to reason dogmatically, reaching extreme and unpalatable conclusions from fairly innocuous premises.

Ibn Abdulwahab was an uncreative anomaly, fearsome, antagonistic, isolated, and ultimately pitiable. At his best, he was a tragic, patrician figure defying and denouncing a duplicitous and base world into which he had been incongruously born, looking unto the past for inspiration. At his worst, he was an obstinate, self-deceived diehard, hurling curses at the wondrous tolerance of the age whose benefits he was too obstinate to recognise and too cold-hearted to appreciate.

## The Jihad Against Television

A fascinating example of the degree to which Ibn Abdulwahab's teachings have permeated the Gulf can be found in the realm of technology.  In the nascent years of television, circa the 1960s, the innovative technology engendered considerable controversy among the religious scholars of Saudi Arabia. Thus they decided to draw upon Ibn Abdulwahab's austere interpretation of *bid'ah,* or religious innovation, to which some scholars opined that television, as an unfamiliar and modern contraption, constituted a deviation from the pristine purity of Islamic teachings and was thus impermissible (Kraidy, 2006). Sheikh Abdulaziz Bin Baz, who was perhaps the most notable scholar of this era, thereby issued a fatwa denouncing television as a pernicious instrument and declared that it corrupted morals and propagated sin (Ayalon, 1995). In his fatwa, he asserted that television was a tool of the devil, which distracted the faithful from their religious duties and encouraged them to engage in illicit activities. In response to this theological opposition, King Faisal of Saudi Arabia, a proponent of modernisation, sought to assuage the concerns of the religious establishment by inviting a delegation of prominent scholars to the Royal Palace for a demonstration of the marvellousness of television and showcasing its potential as an instrument for religious education and the dissemination of Islamic values (Ayalon, 1995). This endeavor gradually allayed the apprehensions of some religious scholars, who came to perceive television as a medium that indeed could be harnessed for the promotion of Islam and the Wahhabi tenets.

Herodotus, in the third book of his Histories, identified her with Aphrodite Urania. Her sanctuary at Ṭāʾif contained a sacred stone made of white granite, where it is said that the Thaqīf habitually offered her sacrifices and in times of drought, libations of milk and honey. Hubal, the great idol of the Kaaba (for whom there were 360 other idols but he stood tallest) was in all probability not of native Arabian origin but an importation from the north, his name perhaps a corruption of ha-Baʿl, 'the Lord', a title of the Canaanite Baal. A humanoid statue carved from red agate (or carnelian) and contained a broken arm which his cult, Quraysh among them, repaired with gold, and before it the arrows of divination were cast. Shayʿ al-Qawm, 'the Companion of the People', or more perhaps properly 'the Escort of the Tribe', was the god of the Nabataean and Safaitic nomads, the protector of caravans and desert travellers, invoked in countless rock inscriptions from the Hawrān to the Wādī Ramm. Strangely, the texts, I may add, repeatedly assert that he 'does not drink wine', a rather curious prohibition which perhaps suggests that his worshippers were not entirely always so abstemious. And finally there were the foreign deities such as Isis, who unlike the others, instantiate only in particular regional, political or mercantile contexts, borne into the Hejaz and the Nabataean kingdom along the caravan routes from Egypt and the Mediterranean.
